The Provenance Table · Dinner Zero

The Pacific Migration Trail

Eaten in one night — Taiwan to Aotearoa
Sixteen seats · one table · seven stops
Farrer Cove · Indian Arm — səl̓ilw̓ət
Arriving by waka from Rocky Point Pier, Port Moody
A canon dinner · executive chef Garth Greenlees

Five thousand years and four thousand miles of open water, sailed in the order it was first made. One fire is struck at the first stop and tended at the centre of the table all night — the same fire that opens the earth at the last. Each pour is offered twice: the drink the voyagers carried, and its counterpart from the cellar.

Prologue · The Crossing

The Embarkation

Guests cross the water to the landing by waka, as the voyagers came — by canoe, from the sea. At the shore the fire is struck. A bound bundle waits at each place: taro slip, breadfruit cutting, a banked coal — the cargo a people would die to keep alive.

To beginGreen coconut, opened at the table and shared — the water that kept people alive on the ocean
Stop One
Taiwan
Origin · where the trail begins
Smoke & the First Ferment
Highland-smoked wild boar, shaved over millet; a foraged bitter green; cured pork aged in the manner of the Bunun and Atayal. The larder before the ocean — smoke as the first refrigeration, ferment as the first chemistry.
The carried pourWarm millet rice wine — the oldest deliberate ferment on the trail
The counterpartKimoto junmai sake, served warm — the same wild rice-ferment lineage, refined a thousand years on
Poured to each guest in turn, eldest first — the first ferment honoured as the elder it is.
Stop Two
The Philippines
The first branching · the islands fill
Kinilaw, Cured at the Table
Line-caught fish cut to order and cured before you in cane vinegar and calamansi, finished with coconut and a breath of chilli — the acid cure that predates ceviche by centuries. The raw-fish thread begins here.
The carried pourTuba — fresh coconut palm wine, from bamboo
The counterpartSkin-contact white, high-acid and saline — cutting the coconut, lifting the cure
No cutlery is set. Eaten kamayan, bare-handed off the leaf — the oldest table manners in the Pacific.
Stop Three
Indonesia
The crossroads · the fermentation heart
Rendang & the Bloom
Beef cooked down in coconut and spice until it is dark and nearly eternal — the travel food of the migrating Minangkabau — beside a sliver of grilled tempeh, the living ferment shown whole.
The carried pourBrem — Balinese fermented rice wine
The counterpartOld-vine Grenache or a warm-climate Syrah — weight and spice to meet the rendang
A censer of whole Maluku clove and nutmeg is lit and carried around the table — the spice the world crossed oceans to seize, passed over each guest.
Stop Four
Melanesia
The deep water · Fiji · the open ocean
Kokoda & the Lovo
Fijian raw fish in coconut cream and lime, then a single parcel drawn from the lovo — the earth oven set into the ground, the same method as the fire on the table, the technology that will end the night.
The ceremonyKava — the yaqona rite, the tanoa bowl, the bound order of service, the single breath
The counterpartBlanc de blancs, brut nature — clean bubbles against the coconut fat, a still room and a rising glass
The room goes still and formal. The oldest beverage ceremony on the trail, observed in earnest.
Stop Five
Polynesia
The hub · Tonga · Samoa · the still point
Lu Pulu & the Leaf
Taro leaf and coconut cream cooked slow into the savoury heart of the trail — deep and restful after the ceremony. The hub is where the migration paused a thousand years; the plate is a pause.
The carried pourʻOtai — chilled pounded fruit and coconut
The counterpartAged Riesling, off-dry — the lift and length to carry the leaf and coconut
The lights fall to dark; only the table fire burns. The star path is spoken — the navigation that launched the two greatest voyages in human history from this exact point.
Stop Six
Hawaiʻi
The fulcrum · ancient larder & melting pot
Two Plates, Two Eras
First the ancient — poke with limu and roasted kukui, the raw-fish thread at its northern reach. Then, seconds later, the melting pot: one exact bite of the plantation century, where five migrations meet on a single plate.
The carried pourʻAwa — the northern dialect of the root, served simply now the rite is behind us
The counterpartJunmai ginjo sake, chilled, for the ancient bite; an ice-cold lager for the camps
Ancient and modern land seconds apart — two thousand years of change tasted in one mouth.
Stop Seven
Aotearoa
Journey’s end · the earth is opened
From the Hāngī
The earth oven, buried before you arrived, is unearthed at the table — the lid of earth lifted, the steam released, kaimoana and the food of the cold country drawn smoking from the ground. The same fire struck at Taiwan, opening the soil at the end of the world.
The carried pourNative botanical infusion — kawakawa, horopito, mānuka — poured hot from the steam of the oven
The counterpartCentral Otago Pinot Noir — the southernmost great red on earth, for the southernmost plate on the trail
Silence as the earth is lifted. The whole table leans in.
The Turn
The Kūmara

One last thing. Everything tonight came down the trail — west, then south, out of Taiwan. This did not. The kūmara is a New World plant, carried home across the Pacific by these same voyagers, who reached the Americas and returned, centuries before any European crossed this ocean. The trail you have just travelled ran both ways. The map was never a line — it was a web, and these were the people who wove it.

The fire, lit at Taiwan and carried all night, is let go out. A last pour of the millet wine, so the end rhymes with the beginning. You have travelled the longest road in human history, and come ashore.

We gather on the land and water of the Tsleil-Waututh — the people of the inlet — whose name is the name of this water, səl̓ilw̓ət, and who have voyaged and fed themselves from it since long before the first of tonight’s stops was sailed. We eat here with their welcome and our thanks.
