## NZ-6 — Pāua (NZ Abalone)

**Cuisine:** Māori / NZ | Shellfish | Difficulty: 2 | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Pāua (Haliotis iris, NZ black-footed abalone) is the most culturally significant shellfish in Aotearoa. The iridescent blue-green shell is used in Māori carving (whakairo) — the eyes of carved ancestral figures on marae wharenui are pāua shell. The meat is prised from intertidal rocks at low tide, traditionally by hand or with a blunt knife. Pāua gathering is governed by strict quota: the recreational daily limit is 10 per person, minimum shell length 125 mm. Rāhui (temporary closures) protect stocks. For coastal iwi — particularly Ngāi Tahu in the South Island and Ngāti Porou on the East Cape — pāua gathering is a whakapapa right, tied to specific rohe moana (sea territories). Al Brown's pāua ravioli at Logan Brown in Wellington became the iconic modern expression: Māori shellfish in Italian pasta.

**Description:**
The pāua foot is removed from the shell and the gut sac discarded. For raw consumption: sliced thin against the grain and eaten immediately — the texture is firm, almost crunchy, with a clean ocean flavour. For tenderising: the foot is pounded with a meat mallet until it yields — traditionally on a rock at the shore. Pounded pāua is then sliced and pan-fried in butter for 30 seconds per side maximum. For mincing: the foot is put through a mincer and used in fritters, patties, or ravioli filling. Overcooking is the universal error — pāua becomes rubber at anything beyond minimal heat. The shell, once the meat is removed, is cleaned and polished for carving or jewellery.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Pāua connects to abalone traditions globally: awabi in Japan (Haliotis discus), Australian abalone (Haliotis rubra), California abalone (Haliotis rufescens, now protected), South African perlemoen (Haliotis midae). The preparation philosophy is universal — minimal cooking, the shellfish's own flavour is the point. Pāua is to NZ what ʻopihi is to Hawaiʻi: the most prized, most regulated, most culturally charged shellfish.

**Flavour Context:**
Raw pāua: firm, clean, intensely oceanic, with a mineral sweetness. Fried pāua (pounded): the butter adds richness, the brief searing adds a nutty caramelised edge, the interior stays briny and tender. Pāua fritters: the minced meat mixed with onion, egg, and breadcrumb, fried golden — a NZ bach (holiday cottage) classic. Al Brown's pāua ravioli: the minced meat in pasta with garlic, herbs, and butter — the ocean in a European package. Pairs with lemon, butter, garlic, NZ Sauvignon Blanc.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Pāua gathered personally at a minus tide, eaten raw within minutes on the rocks. The temperature of the ocean is still in the meat. GOOD: Same-day gathered, pounded and pan-fried in butter — 30 seconds per side, no more. ADEQUATE: Minced into fritters or ravioli — the grinding masks freshness slightly but the flavour remains. INSUFFICIENT: Pāua cooked more than 60 seconds per side — it becomes chewy, then rubbery, then inedible.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: fresh pāua meat is dark (almost black foot), with a firm, glistening surface. If the surface is dull or dry, it is not fresh. Aroma: clean ocean, kelp, mineral — no ammonia. Texture: raw should have a firm snap when bitten; pounded and fried should be tender with slight resistance. Feel: when pounding, the meat should gradually yield under the mallet — if it stays rigid, it needs more pounding; if it tears, it has been over-pounded.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Thirty seconds. Pan-fried pāua has a 30-second window per side. Under that, the surface doesn't caramelise. Over that, the protein tightens and the pāua turns to rubber. There is no recovery from overcooking. The cook watches for the edge of the slice to just curl and turn opaque — that is the moment to flip. Ten seconds later, it is on the plate.

**Doors:**
→ Al Brown's pāua ravioli at Logan Brown: Māori shellfish + Italian technique = modern NZ fine dining. → NZ-14 Al Brown
→ Pāua connects to Japanese awabi, Australian abalone, Hawaiian ʻopihi — the global abalone tradition. → HI-79, JP-[awabi]
→ The pāua shell in Māori carving: food and art from the same animal. The eyes of the ancestors are pāua. → NZ-42b Rongomaraeroa

## NZ-7 — Kina (NZ Sea Urchin)

**Cuisine:** Māori (coastal iwi) | Shellfish | Difficulty: 1 | Time: immediate

**Origin:**
Kina (Evechinus chloroticus, NZ sea urchin) is endemic to Aotearoa — found nowhere else on earth. It is the most divisive food in NZ: those who love it eat it obsessively; those who don't cannot be persuaded. Kina is gathered from rocky reefs by free-diving or at very low tides. The spiny shell is cracked open and the five strips of golden roe (gonads) are scooped out and eaten raw, at the water's edge. No preparation. No accompaniment. This is the purest expression of kaimoana — the ocean, unmediated. Kina was a staple for coastal Māori and remains culturally central to iwi with coastal rohe (territories), particularly Ngāi Tahu, Ngāti Porou, and Ngāpuhi.

**Description:**
The kina shell is cracked open with a rock or knife — strike at the mouth (the flat underside). The five strips of roe are visible inside, ranging from bright golden-orange (prime condition, pre-spawning) to pale cream (post-spawning, inferior). The roe is scooped out with a finger or a small shell and eaten immediately. Prime kina roe is creamy, sweet, intensely briny, with a custard-like texture. In restaurants (Monique Fiso's Hiakai served kina panna cotta), the roe is used as a flavouring agent — folded into custard, added to pasta, or served on toast. But the purist method is the one practised by coastal Māori for centuries: crack, scoop, eat.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Kina connects directly to Japanese uni (sea urchin roe) — same animal class (Echinoidea), same preparation philosophy (raw, minimal), different species. Japanese uni from Hokkaido (Mesocentrotus nudus and Strongylocentrotus intermedius) is globally the benchmark; NZ kina (Evechinus chloroticus) is a distinct species with a different flavour profile — sweeter, less sulphurous, with a cleaner finish. Kina also connects to the sea urchin traditions of Chile, Galicia (Spain), Puglia (Italy), and Jeju Island (Korea).

**Flavour Context:**
Prime kina roe: sweet, briny, creamy, with an ocean-mineral intensity that saturates the palate. The texture is custard-like — it dissolves on the tongue. Post-spawning kina: watery, bitter, mealy — rejected by anyone who knows kina. The flavour is so intense that it works as a seasoning: Fiso's kina panna cotta uses a small quantity of roe to flavour an entire custard. Pairs with: nothing (the purist approach), or very clean-flavoured vehicles — unsalted toast, neutral pasta, plain custard. Does not pair with strong acids or heavy sauces — they destroy the roe's delicacy.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Golden roe from a pre-spawning kina, cracked open on the rocks, eaten within seconds. The roe should fill the shell, bright orange-gold, firm. GOOD: Same-day gathered, roe scooped into a bowl and served chilled. ADEQUATE: Restaurant-sourced kina roe, served as part of a composed dish. INSUFFICIENT: Kina roe that has been stored more than 24 hours, or pale/watery post-spawning roe served as if it were prime.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the roe must be golden-orange, firm, and plump. Pale, watery, or greyish roe is post-spawning and will taste bitter. Aroma: fresh kina smells of clean ocean and iodine — never of ammonia. Texture: prime roe should hold its shape when scooped but dissolve on the tongue. If it is mealy or grainy, it is past its best.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
The spawning cycle. Kina roe quality is entirely determined by where the animal is in its reproductive cycle. Pre-spawning (when the roe is full of stored energy for reproduction) = golden, sweet, firm, magnificent. Post-spawning = pale, bitter, watery, worthless. The dish lives or dies at the calendar. Local knowledge of when kina are in prime condition at a specific reef is held by experienced gatherers and passed down within whānau (families). There is no substitute for this knowledge.

**Doors:**
→ Kina (NZ, Evechinus chloroticus) connects to Japanese uni (Hokkaido, Mesocentrotus nudus). Same animal class, different species, same philosophy: raw, immediate, minimal. → JP-[uni]
→ Fiso's kina panna cotta at Hiakai: the most innovative modern use of kina. → NZ-12 Monique Fiso
→ Kina connects to the kaimoana tradition. → NZ-2

## NZ-8 — Kawakawa (Māori Pepper Tree)

**Cuisine:** Māori | Native Spice / Medicine | Difficulty: 1 | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Kawakawa (Piper excelsum, NZ pepper tree) is the most widely used plant in rongoā Māori (traditional medicine) and one of the foundational spices of the native pantry. The heart-shaped leaves have a warm, peppery, slightly anise-like flavour. The plant belongs to the Piperaceae family — the same family as black pepper (Piper nigrum) and long pepper (Piper longum). Kawakawa is to Māori cuisine what kawakawa's close relative — kava (Piper methysticum) — is to Polynesian ceremony: a Piper-family plant with cultural significance that runs deeper than flavour. The most prized kawakawa leaves are those with insect holes — the holes indicate the caterpillar of the kawakawa looper moth (Cleora scriptaria) has fed on the leaf, which some practitioners believe increases the plant's production of beneficial compounds.

**Description:**
Kawakawa leaves are used fresh or dried. Fresh leaves are brewed into tea — the most common traditional use, drunk for digestive complaints, colds, and general wellness. Dried and ground kawakawa is used as a seasoning — it has a warm, peppery heat milder than horopito (NZ-4) but with a distinctive anise-like backnote. Fiso uses kawakawa in syrups, infusions, and as a finishing herb. OCHO Chocolate in Dunedin pairs kawakawa with horopito in their native-spice chocolate bar. The berries (small, orange-yellow, produced on female plants only) are also edible — peppery and aromatic, used sparingly as a garnish.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Kawakawa (Piper excelsum) is the southernmost member of the Piperaceae family — the same family that gives the world black pepper (India/SE Asia), long pepper (India/Indonesia), cubeb (Java), and kava (Polynesia). The family runs the Pacific Migration Trail: kava (Piper methysticum) is the ceremonial beverage from Fiji to Hawaiʻi; kawakawa is the culinary and medicinal expression in Aotearoa. Same family, different species, different cultural roles — ceremony vs. kitchen.

**Flavour Context:**
Kawakawa: warm, gently peppery, with an anise-like or slightly numbing backnote. Milder and more complex than horopito's direct polygodial hit. Kawakawa tea: warming, peppery, soothing — the NZ equivalent of a peppermint or ginger tea but with more depth. As a seasoning: complements fish (particularly smoked fish), venison, and root vegetables. The kawakawa + horopito combination is the native pepper duo — used together, they provide a layered heat: kawakawa's warm front and horopito's sharp peak.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Fresh kawakawa leaves picked that morning, with insect holes (indicating high bioactive compound production). Brewed immediately into tea or used as a finishing herb. GOOD: Freshly dried kawakawa, stored properly in a sealed container away from light. ADEQUATE: Commercially dried kawakawa from a known supplier (Great Taste NZ). INSUFFICIENT: Old, stale dried kawakawa with no perceptible flavour — kawakawa loses its volatile oils within months if stored improperly.

**Sensory Tests:**
Aroma: fresh kawakawa leaves rubbed between the fingers should release a warm, peppery, slightly anise-scented oil. If there is no aroma, the leaves are too old. Visual: the best leaves are heart-shaped, dark green, with characteristic insect holes. Uniform, hole-free leaves are acceptable but considered less potent by traditional practitioners. Texture: fresh leaves should be supple and slightly waxy; dried leaves should crumble cleanly, not bend (bending indicates residual moisture and potential mould).

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Freshness. Kawakawa's volatile oils dissipate rapidly after harvest. A kawakawa tea made from leaves picked that morning tastes fundamentally different from one made with week-old leaves. The plant grows wild throughout the North Island and coastal South Island — there is no excuse for using old kawakawa. The dish lives at the moment of picking.

**Doors:**
→ Kawakawa (Piper excelsum) is the southernmost Piperaceae — same family as black pepper and kava. The pepper family runs the entire Pacific Migration Trail. → FJ-[kava], WS-[kava]
→ Kawakawa + horopito = the native pepper duo. → NZ-4 Horopito
→ OCHO Chocolate's horopito & kawakawa bar. → NZ-4

## NZ-9 — Pikopiko (NZ Native Fern Fronds)

**Cuisine:** Māori | Native Vegetable | Difficulty: 1 | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Pikopiko is the Māori name for the unfurling fronds (fiddleheads) of several NZ native fern species — most commonly kiokio (Blechnum novae-zelandiae, palm-leaf fern) and ponga (Cyathea dealbata, silver fern). The tightly coiled young fronds are harvested in spring when they are tender and bright green. Pikopiko was a staple green vegetable for Māori before European contact — one of very few indigenous leaf vegetables in NZ. Monique Fiso's dry-aged cold-smoked kahawai with pickled pikopiko was a signature dish at Hiakai. Pikopiko is now supplied commercially by Great Taste NZ (Little Karoo Ltd, Auckland) and foraged by specialist wild-food gatherers.

**Description:**
The coiled fiddlehead fronds are picked when still tightly curled or just beginning to unfurl — once they open, they become bitter and fibrous. The brown papery scales on the outside are rubbed off. Pikopiko is eaten raw in salads (mild, vegetal, with a slight asparagus-like flavour), blanched and dressed (the most common preparation), pickled (Fiso's method — vinegar-pickled pikopiko is used as a garnish and condiment), or sautéed briefly in butter. The flavour is green, clean, slightly nutty, with a texture between asparagus and green bean.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Fiddlehead ferns are eaten across the Pacific and Asia: warabi (bracken fern shoots) in Japan, pako in the Philippines, pakis in Indonesia, and fiddlehead ferns (Matteuccia struthiopteris) in Canada and the northeastern United States. The NZ species are distinct but the culinary concept is universal — the young, coiled frond of a fern harvested at the moment of maximum tenderness before it unfurls.

**Flavour Context:**
Raw pikopiko: mild, green, slightly nutty, with a delicate crunch. Blanched: the flavour concentrates, becoming more vegetal and asparagus-like. Pickled: the vinegar adds brightness, making pikopiko an excellent counterpoint to rich or smoky dishes (hence Fiso's pairing with cold-smoked kahawai). Sautéed in butter: nutty, sweet, with a slight snap. Pairs with: smoked fish, venison, egg dishes, and as a garnish for rich preparations.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Spring-harvested pikopiko, picked that morning, still tightly coiled. Served raw in a salad or blanched within hours. GOOD: Pikopiko harvested within 48 hours, properly refrigerated. ADEQUATE: Commercially supplied pikopiko (Great Taste NZ). INSUFFICIENT: Pikopiko harvested after the fronds have begun to unfurl — bitter, fibrous, and past its season.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the frond should be tightly coiled (the "fiddlehead" shape), bright green, with no browning or wilting. If the coil is opening, it is past its best. Texture: the stem should snap cleanly when bent — if it bends without breaking, it is fibrous and too mature. Aroma: fresh pikopiko has a mild, green, slightly earthy smell — if it smells musty, it is too old.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
The harvest window. Pikopiko is edible for a brief period in spring when the frond is coiled. Too early and there is not enough to eat; too late and the frond is bitter and tough. The window is approximately two weeks per species per location. Local knowledge of where pikopiko grows and when it is ready is essential — this is foraging knowledge held within whānau and passed between generations.

**Doors:**
→ Pikopiko connects to fiddlehead fern traditions globally: Japanese warabi, Filipino pako, Canadian fiddleheads. → JP-[warabi]
→ Fiso's pickled pikopiko with cold-smoked kahawai: the Hiakai signature that put pikopiko on fine-dining plates. → NZ-12, NZ-23
→ Pikopiko comes from the same plant family as mamaku (NZ-16) — different species, same forest, different parts eaten. → NZ-16

DB: difficulty:1|time:varies|species:Blechnum novae-zelandiae, Cyathea dealbata|related:NZ-12,NZ-16,NZ-23

## NZ-10 — Toroi (Fermented Mussels)

**Cuisine:** Māori | Fermented Preservation | Difficulty: 3 | Time: weeks

**Origin:**
Toroi is a traditional Māori fermented food — green-lipped mussels (Perna canaliculus, kūtai) preserved in their own juice mixed with pūhā (Sonchus oleraceus, sow thistle) juice. The mussels are steamed open, the meat is removed and packed with pūhā juice into gourds or containers, and the mixture is left to ferment. The result is a pungent, deeply savoury, umami-rich paste that was one of the primary preservation methods for coastal Māori — allowing protein to be stored and transported inland. Toroi is one of the least-known Māori food traditions outside of Māori communities. It has no commercial expression and is rarely seen in restaurants. It is included here because it represents the preservation axis of Māori foodways — a tradition as sophisticated as any Asian fermented seafood paste.

**Description:**
Green-lipped mussels are gathered (kūtai, from intertidal rocks or mussel beds in the Marlborough Sounds, Coromandel, or similar). They are steamed open over a fire. The meat is extracted and packed tightly into a container with pūhā juice (the sow thistle is pounded and the juice extracted). The container is sealed and left to ferment for several weeks. The fermentation produces lactic acid, preserving the protein while developing intense umami flavour. The finished toroi ranges from mildly fermented (a few days — still recognisably mussel, with a sour tang) to deeply fermented (weeks — a dark, pungent paste with powerful savoury depth). It is eaten as a condiment, mixed into boil-up, or spread on rēwena paraoa (NZ-5).

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Toroi connects to every fermented seafood tradition on the Pacific rim: Filipino bagoong (fermented shrimp paste), Indonesian terasi (fermented shrimp paste), Thai nam pla (fermented fish sauce), Japanese shiokara (fermented squid viscera), Korean jeotgal (fermented seafood). The principle is identical — protein + salt + time → preserved umami. Toroi is the Māori expression of this universal thread. The specific combination of mussel + pūhā is specific to Aotearoa.

**Flavour Context:**
Mildly fermented toroi: sour, briny, with recognisable mussel flavour. Deeply fermented toroi: intensely savoury, umami-rich, pungent, with a complexity comparable to aged fish sauce. The pūhā juice contributes a bitter-green backnote. Used as a condiment: a small amount of toroi adds deep savoury depth to soups, stews, and boil-up. On rēwena bread: the sour tang of the bread complements the fermented savoury of the toroi.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Toroi made from fresh kūtai gathered that day, fermented with hand-pounded pūhā juice, aged for 3+ weeks in a controlled environment. Made by a practitioner with generational knowledge. GOOD: Toroi from fresh mussels, properly fermented for at least 7 days. ADEQUATE: Short-fermented toroi (2–3 days) — milder, less complex. INSUFFICIENT: Unfermented mussel preparation called "toroi" — without the fermentation, it is not toroi.

**Sensory Tests:**
Aroma: properly fermented toroi should smell pungent and savoury — like aged fish sauce — not putrid or rotten. The line is fine but clear. Trained practitioners know the difference. Visual: the colour darkens with fermentation — from mussel-grey to amber-brown. If it turns black or shows mould (other than controlled surface mould), the fermentation has failed. Texture: mildly fermented toroi has recognisable mussel texture; deeply fermented toroi becomes a paste.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
The fermentation environment. Toroi needs a cool (not cold), stable environment to ferment properly. Too warm and the fermentation runs wild — producing off-flavours and potential spoilage. Too cold and the fermentation stalls. Coastal Māori fermented toroi in the moderate temperatures of NZ's maritime climate. Modern attempts in centrally heated kitchens often fail because the temperature fluctuates too much. The dish lives at the consistency of its fermentation environment.

**Doors:**
→ Toroi connects to the global fermented-seafood tradition: Filipino bagoong, Thai nam pla, Japanese shiokara, Korean jeotgal. → PH-[bagoong], JP-[shiokara]
→ The mussel is the green-lipped mussel — endemic to NZ (Perna canaliculus). → NZ-25 Kūtai
→ Toroi on rēwena paraoa: fermented mussel on fermented bread. Two Māori fermentation traditions on one plate. → NZ-5

DB: difficulty:3|time:weeks|species:Perna canaliculus, Sonchus oleraceus|related:NZ-5,NZ-25,PH-bagoong

## NZ-11 — Whitebait (Īnanga)

**Cuisine:** NZ (South Island especially) | Freshwater Fish | Difficulty: 1 | Time: 5–10 minutes

**Origin:**
NZ whitebait (primarily Galaxias maculatus, īnanga) is the most passionately debated ingredient in NZ food culture. The whitebait season (August 15 to November 30 on the West Coast of the South Island) is a national event. Whitebaiters stake out river-mouth stands before dawn, competing for position with the intensity of an arms race. The tiny, translucent juvenile fish are caught in fine-mesh scoop nets as they run upriver from the sea. NZ whitebait are different from European whitebait — they are the juveniles of five galaxiid species, not herring or sprat. The West Coast of the South Island (Hokitika, Greymouth, Westport) is the heartland of whitebaiting culture. Prices reach NZ$100–150 per kilogram in season. Outside NZ, few people have tasted them.

**Description:**
The classic NZ whitebait fritter: fresh whitebait, one egg, a pinch of salt, and nothing else — no flour, no onion, no herbs. The batter is just enough egg to bind the fish. Spoonfuls are dropped into a hot pan with butter and fried until golden on both sides — approximately 2 minutes per side. The fritter is served on white bread with a squeeze of lemon and butter. That is the canonical preparation. Any addition beyond egg and salt is considered heresy by West Coast traditionalists. Whitebait are also eaten in omelets, on toast, or (rarely) in tempura, but the fritter is the benchmark.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
NZ whitebait connects to tiny-fish traditions globally: Italian bianchetti (banned in the EU since 2006 for conservation), Spanish chanquetes (similarly banned), Japanese shirasu (whitebait), Korean myeolchi (anchovy juveniles), and the British whitebait fritter tradition (herring/sprat juveniles). The NZ preparation is the most minimal on this list — the egg-only fritter is a purity test. The Italian fritto misto adds flour, herbs, and garlic; the NZ fritter rejects all of that.

**Flavour Context:**
Fresh whitebait: delicate, sweet, slightly marine, with a clean river-and-sea character. The flavour is subtle — which is exactly why the classic fritter uses only egg and salt. Any strong flavour overwhelms the whitebait. The egg sets around the fish, creating a lacy golden exterior with the tiny fish visible within. The butter the fritter is fried in contributes richness. The lemon provides a lift. White bread provides a neutral base. NZ Sauvignon Blanc (Marlborough) is the canonical pairing — the wine's herbaceous acidity complements the whitebait's delicacy.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Whitebait caught that morning on the West Coast, fritters made within hours. Egg, salt, butter, pan, bread, lemon. Nothing else. GOOD: Same-day whitebait, properly handled and refrigerated. ADEQUATE: Fresh whitebait purchased from a fishmonger in season (same week). INSUFFICIENT: Frozen whitebait — the delicate texture collapses during freezing; the fritter will be watery and flat.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: fresh whitebait should be translucent with a slight silver sheen, with visible dark eyes. If they are opaque or white, they are not fresh. The fritter should be golden-brown with the individual fish visible in the lacy egg matrix. Aroma: fresh whitebait should smell of clean river water and the sea — no ammonia, no strong fish smell. Texture: the fritter should be crisp on the outside and tender within; the whitebait should have a delicate pop between the teeth.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
The egg. The whitebait fritter has one binding ingredient: egg. If the ratio is wrong — too much egg and the fritter is an omelet with fish in it; too little and the fritter falls apart in the pan — the dish fails. The correct ratio: approximately one egg per 100g of whitebait. The fish should dominate; the egg should be invisible infrastructure. The fritter lives or dies at the egg ratio.

**Doors:**
→ NZ whitebait connects to Italian bianchetti, Spanish chanquetes, Japanese shirasu — tiny-fish traditions that are disappearing globally due to overfishing. → IT-[bianchetti], JP-[shirasu]
→ The West Coast whitebaiting culture is uniquely NZ — the river-mouth stands, the dawn starts, the territorial disputes. → NZ-[West Coast regional]
→ Whitebait fritter on white bread with butter and lemon: the most minimal preparation in the NZ canon. → NZ-2 Kaimoana (minimal preparation philosophy)
