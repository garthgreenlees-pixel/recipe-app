## WS-1 — Umu (Samoan Earth Oven)

**Cuisine:** Samoan | Earth Oven | Difficulty: 3 | Time: 3–6 hours (pit preparation + cooking)

**Origin:**
The umu is Samoa's earth oven — the central cooking method of Samoan culture and the direct Polynesian cognate of Fijian lovo (FJ-1), Tongan umu, Hawaiian imu (HI-1), and Māori hāngi (NZ-1). The Samoan umu is fired every Sunday for the to'ona'i (Sunday family meal after church) — a weekly ritual that has survived colonisation, Christianisation, and modernisation. The umu is prepared by the taulele'a (untitled young men of the village), who are responsible for gathering stones, cutting firewood, and tending the fire. Women prepare the food — peeling taro, scraping coconut, wrapping palusami. This division of labour is codified in fa'a Samoa (the Samoan way) and has not changed in substance for centuries. Robert Oliver documents the Sunday umu extensively in Mea'ai Samoa (Random House, 2013) and Me'a Kai (2010). The Samoan umu differs from its corridor neighbours in two technical respects: the stones are river basalt (not volcanic in origin but dense enough to hold heat), and the wrapping material is primarily banana leaf and breadfruit leaf rather than the ti leaf of Hawaiʻi or the harakeke of Aotearoa.

**Description:**
A pit is dug approximately 60–90 cm deep (shallower than a Fijian lovo, which tends to be larger and deeper). River stones are arranged in a bed at the base. Coconut husks and hardwood logs are stacked above the stones and fired. Burning time: 1–2 hours until the stones are white-hot. Excess coals are raked aside. Food is arranged in layers: root vegetables (taro, breadfruit, banana) go directly on the stones. Proteins (whole pig, chicken, fish) go next. Palusami parcels (WS-3) are placed on top, where the heat is gentlest. The umu is covered with banana leaves and breadfruit leaves in overlapping layers, then woven coconut-frond mats, then a final layer of leaves. No earth is used to seal the Samoan umu — this is a key distinction from the Māori hāngi and Hawaiian imu, which use earth. The absence of earth covering means the Samoan umu loses heat faster and cooks at a lower sustained temperature, producing a gentler, more steamed result. Cooking time: 2–4 hours for a standard Sunday to'ona'i.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Earth-oven thread: TW-2 → Indonesian barapen → PNG-1 (mumu) → FJ-1 (lovo) → WS-1 (Samoan umu) → TO-1 (Tongan umu) → HI-1 (Hawaiian imu) → NZ-1 (hāngi). The Samoan umu is technically closest to the Tongan umu (both use the same wrapping materials and do not use earth to seal), but distinct from the Hawaiian imu (earth-sealed, producing more intense heat and smoky character) and the Māori hāngi (earth-sealed, using volcanic stones from geothermal regions). The Samoan umu is the gentlest of the Polynesian earth ovens — lower heat, shorter cooking time, more reliance on steam than radiant heat. → Related: FJ-1, TO-1, HI-1, NZ-1, PNG-1, TW-2

**Flavour Context:**
The Samoan umu produces a cleaner, more delicate flavour than its earth-sealed cousins. Without earth covering, there is less mineral and smoky character. The dominant flavour comes from the banana-leaf wrapping (a faint, sweet vegetal note), the slow-rendered fat of the protein (pork, in particular, develops a silky, meltingly tender quality), and the caramelised starch of the root vegetables. Taro cooked in the umu develops a purple-grey interior and a dense, sticky texture. Breadfruit (Artocarpus altilis) cooked in the umu develops a nutty, chestnut-like character. The coconut cream of the palusami parcels reduces and concentrates during cooking, producing a rich, sweet sauce that permeates the taro leaves. Species: Samoan taro (talo, Colocasia esculenta) and ta'amu (giant swamp taro, Alocasia macrorrhizos) are both used.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: village umu for a fa'alavelave (ceremonial occasion) — whole pig, fresh-caught reef fish, multiple taro varieties, freshly made palusami. Reserve: Sunday to'ona'i with a taulele'a team that has been preparing umu for years — practised stone selection, precise heat management. Market: family umu with mixed proteins and store-bought ingredients. House: commercial "umu-style" cooking in a conventional oven — it mimics the wrapping but cannot replicate the stone-heat convection and steam environment.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: properly cooked umu taro has a uniform purple-grey colour throughout, with no white or hard centre. Umu pork has a golden-amber skin (if whole pig) with meat that pulls apart in long fibres. Aroma: opening the umu releases steam carrying banana-leaf sweetness, rendered pork fat, and the earthy scent of cooked taro. Texture: umu taro should be sticky and dense — it clings to the fingers. Umu pork should shred without resistance. Feel: the steam from a freshly opened umu is intensely hot and humid; this moisture is part of the cooking mechanism.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
The umu lives or dies on stone selection and heat management. River stones that are too porous absorb water and can crack (or rarely, explode) when heated. Dense basalt is essential. The second pivot: timing the removal of coals. If too many coals are left in the pit, the bottom layer of food (root vegetables) burns and chars. If too few, the umu loses heat before the proteins are cooked through. The taulele'a learns this timing through apprenticeship — there is no written recipe. A miscalculated umu produces undercooked taro (hard, starchy, and inedible) and raw-centred pork — a failure that is publicly visible at the to'ona'i.

## WS-2 — Oka (Samoan Raw Fish in Coconut Cream)

**Cuisine:** Samoan | Raw Fish | Difficulty: 1 | Time: 30–45 minutes (marination)

**Origin:**
Oka is Samoa's expression of the Pacific raw-fish-in-coconut-cream tradition — fresh fish (reef fish or tuna) diced, marinated briefly in lime or lemon juice, then dressed in coconut cream with onion, tomato, chilli, and salt. It is the Samoan cognate of Fijian kokoda (FJ-2), Tongan 'ota ika (TO-3), Cook Islands ika mata (CK-2), and Tahitian poisson cru (TP-1). The name oka is sometimes spelled oka i'a (oka = raw/uncooked, i'a = fish). Oliver documents oka as the most eaten raw-fish preparation in Samoa, appearing at every level from roadside stall to high-end resort (Mea'ai Samoa, 2013). The Samoan preparation is distinguished from its Fijian neighbour by a higher heat level (more chilli) and a preference for skipjack tuna (atu, Katsuwonus pelamis) over reef fish. The cultural position of oka in Samoa is equivalent to poke in Hawaiʻi — it is the default way to eat fresh fish.

**Description:**
Fresh fish is filleted, skinned, and cut into 1–2 cm cubes. Skipjack tuna (atu, Katsuwonus pelamis) is the preferred species; yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares) is also used; reef fish (parrotfish, emperor) are acceptable but considered less prestigious. The cubes are placed in a bowl and covered with fresh lime juice (or lemon). Marination: 15–30 minutes. The acid turns the fish surface opaque white. The juice is drained (some Samoan cooks leave a small amount for added sharpness). Freshly squeezed coconut cream (pe'epe'e, the Samoan term for first-pressing lolo) is added. Diced onion, diced tomato, sliced fresh chilli (Samoan preparations are hotter than Fijian kokoda), chopped cucumber (a modern addition), and salt are folded through. Sea grapes (limu, a type of marine algae in the Caulerpa genus) are sometimes added for texture and salinity. The dish is served immediately at cool temperature.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Raw-fish thread: TW-1 (Amis silaw) → PH-1 (kinilaw) → FJ-2 (kokoda) → WS-2 (oka) → TO-3 ('ota ika) → CK-2 (ika mata) → TP-1 (poisson cru) → HI-4 (poke) → NZ-2 (kaimoana). Oka's distinction from kokoda: the Samoan preparation uses more chilli, prefers tuna over reef fish, and sometimes includes sea grapes (limu). The distinction from 'ota ika (Tonga): the Tongan version is creamier, with more coconut cream relative to acid. The distinction from poisson cru (Tahiti): the Tahitian version uses coconut milk (thinner) rather than cream, and adds lime juice directly to the cream (not drained first). Each variant reflects its island's larder and palate preferences. → Related: FJ-2, TO-3, CK-2, TP-1, HI-4, NZ-2, TW-1, PH-1

**Flavour Context:**
Oka is sharper and more assertive than kokoda. The retained acid (Samoan cooks drain less than Fijians), the higher chilli content, and the mineral salinity of the tuna (compared to milder reef fish) produce a brighter, more muscular dish. The coconut cream rounds the acid and heat into a balanced whole. Key species distinction: skipjack tuna has a richer, more iron-y flavour than the mild white flesh of reef fish — it stands up to the lime and chilli without being overwhelmed. Sea grapes (Caulerpa lentillifera or C. racemosa) contribute a briny pop — small green spheres that burst between the teeth, releasing salt and ocean flavour. The overall profile: bright acid, rich coconut fat, moderate chilli heat, iron-rich fish, salt.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: freshly caught atu (skipjack tuna), same-hour preparation, hand-squeezed pe'epe'e from mature coconut, sea grapes gathered from the reef that morning. Reserve: market-fresh tuna with fresh coconut cream at a family gathering. Market: fresh fish from Apia fish market, fresh or quality canned coconut cream. House: frozen tuna with canned cream — the texture degrades (frozen fish releases water that thins the sauce) but the flavour remains acceptable.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the tuna cubes should be opaque white on the surface with a deep pink-red interior (the centre remains raw). The coconut cream should coat every piece evenly. Aroma: fresh lime, coconut, and a faint metallic-oceanic note from the tuna. Texture: the surface should be firm (acid-denatured) with a yielding, almost buttery centre — two textures in one bite. If uniformly firm throughout, the fish has been over-marinated. Feel: the chilli should produce a slow, building heat on the tongue, not an immediate burn.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Oka lives or dies on fish freshness and marination timing. The fish must be sashimi-grade fresh — caught within 24 hours, stored on ice, with no ammonia smell. Tuna that has been frozen and thawed releases excess moisture that dilutes the coconut cream and produces a watery oka. The marination pivot: 15–30 minutes is the window. Under 15 minutes, the acid has not denatured the surface sufficiently — the coconut cream slides off raw fish. Over 45 minutes, the acid penetrates to the centre, producing a uniformly chalky, dry texture. Samoan cooks judge readiness by squeezing a cube between thumb and forefinger — the surface should feel firm but the centre should yield.

## WS-3 — Palusami (Taro Leaves Stuffed with Coconut Cream)

**Cuisine:** Samoan | Leaf Vegetable / Technique | Difficulty: 2 | Time: 2–4 hours (umu) or 45–60 minutes (oven)

**Origin:**
Palusami is the centrepiece of Samoan cooking — young taro leaves wrapped around a filling of coconut cream and onion, tied into a parcel, and baked in the umu (WS-1). It is Samoa's national dish in all but official designation. The name palusami derives from palu (to mix, to knead) and sami (referring to the mixing action). The technique is the Samoan expression of the taro-leaf-in-coconut-cream thread that runs through the Pacific: Fijian rourou (FJ-3) cooks the leaves loose in cream; Tongan lu (TO-2) wraps leaves around corned beef; Samoan palusami stuffs leaves around a cream-and-onion core. Oliver identifies palusami as "the dish that defines Samoan food" (Mea'ai Samoa, 2013). Every Sunday umu includes palusami. Every ceremonial meal includes palusami. It is the Samoan food that Samoan diaspora communities in Auckland, Sydney, and Los Angeles make when they miss home.

**Description:**
Young taro leaves (lau talo, Colocasia esculenta — the youngest, tenderest leaves from the centre of the plant) are washed and de-stemmed. Each leaf is placed flat, slightly overlapping to form a cup approximately 15 cm in diameter. A tablespoon of diced onion is placed in the centre. Fresh coconut cream (pe'epe'e) is poured over the onion — approximately 3–4 tablespoons per parcel. Salt is added. The leaves are folded over the filling, enclosing it completely. The parcel is wrapped in a second layer of larger taro leaves or banana leaf, then tied with a strip of banana bark or string. The parcels are placed in the umu on the top layer (gentlest heat) and cooked for 2–4 hours. In the oven method: 180°C / 355°F for 45–60 minutes. The result: the taro leaves become silky-soft, the coconut cream reduces into a thick, sweet, onion-infused sauce, and the calcium oxalate in the raw leaves is fully neutralised by the prolonged heat. Variants: some families add corned beef (a Tongan influence), tinned fish (a modern economy addition), or curry powder (an Indian-Fijian influence).

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Taro-leaf thread: TW-4 (cinavu leaf wraps) → FJ-3 (rourou) → WS-3 (palusami) → TO-2 (lu pulu) → HI-5 (laulau). Palusami is the stuffed variant — the Samoan innovation is to use the taro leaf as both vessel and vegetable, with the coconut cream as an internal sauce rather than an external one. The technique of wrapping protein or starch in leaves and cooking in an earth oven is universal across the Pacific and connects to banana-leaf-wrapped preparations in Southeast Asia (Thai hor mok, Filipino tinola sa dahon, Malaysian otak-otak). The structural principle: edible leaf as wrapper + filling + slow heat = self-saucing parcel. → Related: FJ-3, TO-2, HI-5, TW-4

**Flavour Context:**
Palusami has a deep, sweet, creamy flavour dominated by the reduced coconut cream, which concentrates during the 2–4 hours in the umu. The taro leaves contribute an earthy, spinach-like green flavour — but richer and more mineral than spinach, with a slight bitterness that balances the coconut sweetness. The onion caramelises inside the parcel, adding sweetness and depth. The overall flavour profile: sweet (coconut cream + caramelised onion), green (taro leaf), mineral (taro leaf iron content), with a faint smoky undertone from the umu. Species: Colocasia esculenta — the same species used across the Pacific. Young leaves (pale green, unfurled) are preferred for their tenderness and lower oxalate content.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: young taro leaves picked that morning, first-pressing coconut cream, baked 3+ hours in a village umu. This is the palusami that Samoan grandmothers make. Reserve: Sunday to'ona'i palusami with fresh ingredients and a well-managed umu. Market: oven-baked with fresh leaves and fresh coconut cream — good, but lacks the earth-oven steam character. House: canned coconut cream with frozen taro leaves — functional but the leaves do not soften as well from frozen, and the canned cream produces a thinner sauce.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the parcel, when unwrapped, should reveal dark green leaves that have collapsed into a soft mass, surrounding a pool of thick, ivory-gold coconut cream. The onion should be translucent and soft. Aroma: rich coconut, sweet caramelised onion, green-vegetal taro leaf. Texture: the leaves should be silky — no fibre, no resistance, no grittiness. The cream should be thick enough to coat a spoon. If the leaves are tough or the cream is thin, the cooking time was insufficient. Feel: a faint warming sensation from the residual calcium oxalate is acceptable in traditional preparations but indicates the leaves could have been cooked longer.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Palusami lives or dies on leaf selection and cooking time. Old leaves (dark green, large, with thick central veins) contain high concentrations of calcium oxalate and require extended cooking (3+ hours) to neutralise. They also have a tougher texture that may not fully soften. Young leaves (pale green, small, with thin stems) cook faster and produce a more delicate result. The second pivot: coconut cream volume. Each parcel needs enough cream to create a self-saucing environment inside the leaf wrapper. Too little cream produces dry, tough palusami. The cream should pool inside the parcel, bathing the leaves from within.

## WS-4 — Fa'alifu (Samoan Coconut Cream Sauce Technique)

**Cuisine:** Samoan | Sauce Technique | Difficulty: 1 | Time: 15–30 minutes

**Origin:**
Fa'alifu is a Samoan cooking technique — not a single dish — in which vegetables or proteins are simmered in coconut cream until the cream reduces and coats the food. The term fa'alifu (also spelled fa'alifu fa'i when applied to bananas, or fa'alifu talo when applied to taro) describes the action of cooking in lolo (coconut cream) until the cream thickens and begins to caramelise. It is the Samoan expression of the pan-Pacific principle that coconut cream is not a garnish but a cooking medium — a principle shared with Fijian rourou (FJ-3), Tongan lu (TO-2), and Filipino ginataang preparations. Oliver describes fa'alifu as "the method that ties Samoan cooking together" (Mea'ai Samoa, 2013). The technique is applied to green bananas, taro, breadfruit, leafy greens, fish, and chicken — the base method is the same, only the ingredient changes. It is taught to Samoan children as the foundational cooking method alongside the umu.

**Description:**
The ingredient (green banana, taro, breadfruit, leafy greens, or fish) is peeled and cut into pieces. In a pot over medium heat, fresh coconut cream (pe'epe'e, first pressing) is brought to a gentle simmer. The ingredient is added to the simmering cream. Salt is added. The pot is cooked uncovered on low-to-medium heat, stirring occasionally, for 15–30 minutes (vegetables) or 10–15 minutes (fish). The key technical moment: the cream reduces, thickens, and begins to separate slightly — the fat rises to the surface as a clear, golden oil while the solids coat the food. This separation (called the "breaking" of coconut cream) is intentional in fa'alifu — it indicates that the cream has concentrated sufficiently. In Western cooking, separated cream is a failure; in Samoan fa'alifu, it is the goal. The food is served in its cream sauce, with the separated oil pooling on top. Fa'alifu fa'i (green banana): the bananas soften and absorb the cream, becoming creamy and sweet. Fa'alifu talo (taro): the taro develops a sticky, dense texture and a nutty flavour.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Coconut-cream cooking thread: the technique of simmering food in coconut cream until the cream reduces and separates is found across the tropics. Indonesian rendang is the extended form — coconut cream cooked until it separates completely and the protein caramelises in the residual oil. Thai kaeng kati uses a similar reduction. Filipino ginataang follows the same principle (gata = coconut cream). Samoan fa'alifu is the simplest expression: one ingredient, one sauce, no spice paste, no aromatics beyond salt. This minimalism is characteristic of Polynesian cooking — the ingredient and the coconut cream are the entire dish. → Related: FJ-3, FJ-5, TO-2, PH-1

**Flavour Context:**
Fa'alifu has a clean, two-note flavour: the sweetness and fat of the coconut cream, and the flavour of the ingredient. Fa'alifu fa'i: the green banana contributes a starchy, slightly tannic base note that the cream softens into something between banana and potato. Fa'alifu talo: the taro contributes an earthy, nutty flavour that intensifies as the cream reduces. Fa'alifu i'a (fish): the fish releases its own juices into the cream, creating a seafood-coconut broth. The separated coconut oil on top contributes a rich, toasted-coconut aroma. The flavour is defined by restraint — no garlic, no ginger, no chilli (those are modern additions, not traditional). Species: green cooking banana (Musa × paradisiaca, the plantain family) and Colocasia esculenta taro.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: freshly dug taro or just-picked green banana, first-pressing coconut cream from a mature coconut, cooked slowly until the cream separates into a golden oil and a thick, coating sauce. Reserve: Sunday lunch preparation with fresh ingredients. Market: fresh ingredients with quality canned coconut cream. House: frozen or tinned ingredients with canned cream — edible but the cream does not separate as cleanly and the resulting sauce is thinner.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the golden coconut oil pooling on top of the thick white cream coating the food. This oil layer is the sign that fa'alifu has been cooked correctly. If the cream is uniformly white, it has not reduced enough. Aroma: toasted coconut, warm fat, and the vegetal or seafood note of the ingredient. Texture: the food should be soft but not mushy — taro should hold its shape while being creamy throughout. The cream should coat the back of a spoon. Feel: the rich mouthfeel of full-fat coconut cream, with the slippery quality of the separated oil.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Fa'alifu lives or dies on the cream reduction. The cook must allow the coconut cream to simmer long enough for the fat to separate from the protein solids — this takes 15–25 minutes at a gentle simmer. Rushing the process (boiling instead of simmering) causes the cream to break unevenly, producing a grainy, curdled texture instead of a smooth separation. The heat must be low enough that the bottom does not scorch — burnt coconut cream produces a bitter, acrid flavour that cannot be rescued. The second pivot: stirring frequency. Too much stirring prevents the oil from separating cleanly. Too little stirring allows the bottom to stick and burn. The correct frequency is every 3–4 minutes — enough to prevent scorching but not so much that the cream emulsifies back together.

## WS-5 — Sapasui (Samoan Chop Suey)

**Cuisine:** Samoan | Noodle Dish / Trade Thread | Difficulty: 1 | Time: 30–45 minutes

**Origin:**
Sapasui is Samoa's adaptation of Chinese chop suey — bean-thread noodles (cellophane noodles, made from mung bean starch) cooked with soy sauce, ground beef or pork, onion, garlic, and vegetables. The name is a Samoan phonetic rendering of "chop suey." The dish arrived in Samoa via Chinese immigrant traders and labourers in the early 20th century and was rapidly adopted into Samoan communal cooking. Today, sapasui appears at every to'ona'i (Sunday meal) and every fa'alavelave (ceremony) alongside palusami (WS-3) and umu-cooked meats (WS-1). It is considered a Samoan dish, not a Chinese one — the adaptation is complete. The cultural parallel is Filipino pancit (PH-6): Chinese noodle technique absorbed into a Pacific Island culinary identity and made local through ingredient substitution and communal scale. Oliver includes sapasui in Mea'ai Samoa (2013) as an example of how Samoan cuisine absorbs outside influences and transforms them.

**Description:**
Dried bean-thread noodles (also called glass noodles, cellophane noodles, or long rice — made from mung bean starch, Vigna radiata) are soaked in warm water for 10–15 minutes until pliable. Ground beef (or pork mince) is browned in a large pot with diced onion and minced garlic. Soy sauce is added (light soy, generously — Samoan sapasui is darker and saltier than its Chinese ancestor). Water or stock is added. The soaked noodles are added to the pot and simmered for 10–15 minutes until the noodles have absorbed the sauce and become translucent. Sliced cabbage and carrots are added in the last 5 minutes. The dish is served in a large communal bowl. Quantities are generous — sapasui for a family to'ona'i serves 15–20 people from a single pot. The noodles absorb liquid as they cool, becoming stickier and more dense — this is expected and preferred. Leftovers are eaten cold or reheated.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Chinese-trade thread: sapasui connects to every Chinese-influenced noodle adaptation across the Pacific and Southeast Asia. Filipino pancit (PH-6) is the closest parallel — Chinese egg noodles or rice noodles adapted to the local larder. Hawaiian saimin is another expression — a noodle soup with Japanese, Chinese, Filipino, and Hawaiian influences. The bean-thread noodle (mung bean starch) is itself a Chinese ingredient that has been adopted across East and Southeast Asia (Korean japchae, Thai pad woon sen, Vietnamese miến). Sapasui represents the easternmost stop on the Chinese noodle thread's Pacific extension. The technique principle: starch noodle + soy sauce + protein + vegetable, cooked as a one-pot communal dish. → Related: PH-6, HI-4

**Flavour Context:**
Sapasui has a savoury, soy-forward flavour with a sweetness from caramelised onion and a depth from browned meat. The bean-thread noodles contribute no flavour of their own — they are pure starch, absorbing whatever they are cooked in. The soy sauce is the dominant seasoning. Garlic provides a background pungency. Cabbage adds a mild sweetness and crunch. The dish is not spicy — chilli is not a traditional sapasui ingredient (unlike the hotter preparations of Melanesia or the Philippines). The overall profile: salty, savoury, meaty, with the slippery, slightly chewy texture of glass noodles. Species: mung bean noodles (Vigna radiata starch) — not rice noodles, not wheat noodles.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: made with hand-ground fresh beef from a family pig (in Samoan households, beef and pork are interchangeable in this dish), fresh vegetables, and quality soy sauce, cooked slowly so the noodles absorb maximum flavour. Reserve: to'ona'i preparation in large quantity — the communal scale improves the dish (more meat juices, more time for noodle absorption). Market: family kitchen preparation with store-bought ingredients. House: made quickly with instant noodles instead of bean-thread — this is a common shortcut but produces a fundamentally different dish (wheat vs. starch noodle).

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the noodles should be translucent and glossy, deeply coloured from the soy sauce (dark amber to brown). The beef should be visible as small, browned bits distributed through the noodles. Aroma: soy sauce, garlic, and browned meat — a warm, savoury, kitchen-filling smell. Texture: the noodles should be slippery and slightly chewy — a gentle resistance, then a clean bite. If the noodles are mushy, they have been overcooked. If they are still white and opaque, they have not absorbed enough sauce. Feel: the dish should feel warm and comforting — sapasui is Samoan comfort food.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Sapasui lives or dies on noodle hydration and sauce absorption. The bean-thread noodles must be soaked long enough to become pliable (10–15 minutes in warm water) but not so long that they become mushy (more than 30 minutes). Under-soaked noodles remain brittle and snap instead of stretching. Over-soaked noodles dissolve into a starchy paste. The second pivot: liquid ratio. The noodles need enough liquid (water, stock, and soy sauce combined) to absorb during cooking — approximately twice their dry volume. Too little liquid produces dry, clumped noodles. Too much produces a soup instead of a noodle dish. The correct result is moist but not swimming — the noodles should cling together in a cohesive mass.
