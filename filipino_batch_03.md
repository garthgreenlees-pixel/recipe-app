## PH-12 — Pinakbet (Ilocano Vegetable Technique)

**Cuisine:** Filipino — Ilocano / Ilocos Region, Northern Luzon | Regional Technique | Difficulty: 2 | Time: 30–45 min

**Origin:**
Pinakbet (also pakbet) is the Ilocano vegetable technique — a method of cooking a specific combination of vegetables with bagoong (PH-9) as the sole seasoning agent, using minimal liquid and relying on the vegetables' own moisture. The word derives from the Ilocano "pinakebbet," meaning "shrunken" or "shrivelled" — referring to the final state of the vegetables after cooking. Fernandez (Tikim, 1994, ISBN 978-9712703836) identifies pinakbet as the definitive expression of the Ilocano vegetable-forward tradition: in a region where meat was scarce and vegetables abundant, the Ilocano cook developed a technique that makes vegetables the centre of the plate. The vegetable combination is prescribed, not arbitrary: ampalaya (bitter melon, Momordica charantia), kalabasa (squash), sitaw (long beans), talong (eggplant), okra, and tomato. The bitter melon is the critical ingredient — its bitterness is intentional, not an accident. Fernandez notes that Ilocano cuisine deliberately incorporates bitterness as a flavour dimension that other Filipino regional traditions avoid. The bagoong provides salt and umami without adding liquid; the technique is closer to a dry stew than a braise.

**Description:**
The Ilocano method: slice the vegetables into uniform pieces. In a clay pot or heavy pan, heat a small amount of oil. Sautee bagoong alamang (PH-9, 2–3 tablespoons per serving) with crushed garlic, sliced onion, and sliced tomato until fragrant. Layer the vegetables in the pot in a specific order based on cooking time: squash on the bottom (densest, longest cooking), then eggplant, bitter melon, long beans, okra. Do not add water — the vegetables will release their own moisture. Cover tightly. Cook over medium heat for 20–30 min, shaking the pot occasionally (do not stir — the vegetables should maintain their layers and cook in steam). The result: the vegetables are soft but not mushy, coated in the bagoong-garlic-tomato base, with each vegetable contributing its own flavour to the pot. The bitter melon should remain distinctly bitter — it is not cooked to neutralise its bitterness but to integrate it into the dish's flavour profile. Optional protein: pork belly slices or grilled fish may be layered in with the vegetables, but the vegetable version is the canonical Ilocano expression.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Vegetable-as-centre thread: pinakbet connects to the global tradition of vegetable-dominated dishes in regions where meat is scarce or expensive — Italian caponata (eggplant-forward vegetable stew), Provencal ratatouille (vegetable medley relying on each vegetable's character), Japanese nimono (simmered vegetable dishes). The bagoong-as-sole-seasoning thread connects pinakbet to the fermented-paste-seasoned vegetable traditions of Southeast Asia: Thai pad prik khing (vegetables with curry paste), Malaysian sambal vegetables. The bitter melon thread: the deliberate inclusion of bitterness connects to Chinese bitter-melon dishes, Indian karela preparations, and Okinawan goya champuru — all cuisines that treat bitterness as a desirable flavour component rather than a defect. → Related: PH-4, PH-9, PH-13

**Flavour Context:**
The dominant flavour is bitter-savoury: the bitter melon provides the bitterness that defines pinakbet's Ilocano identity, and the bagoong provides the salt-umami-funk that seasons the entire dish. The squash adds sweetness (counterbalancing the bitter melon), the tomato adds acidity, the eggplant adds creaminess, the okra adds a slight mucilaginous body to the sauce. The overall profile: bitter, savoury, sweet, with a fermented-funk undertone from the bagoong — a complex vegetable dish that rewards repeated eating.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: fresh Ilocano vegetables from northern Luzon markets, artisanal bagoong from Pangasinan, cooked in a clay pot over wood fire — the clay-pot technique produces a different steam dynamic than metal pots. Reserve: fresh vegetables with quality bagoong in any pot. Market: supermarket vegetables with commercial bagoong — functional but the freshness and variety of Ilocano market vegetables is absent. House: pinakbet with the bitter melon omitted is not pinakbet — it is sauteed vegetables with bagoong. The bitter melon is the identity ingredient.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the vegetables should be distinct — not homogenised into a mush but retaining their individual colours (green bitter melon, orange squash, purple eggplant, green beans, green okra, red tomato) coated in the pale-pink bagoong dressing. Aroma: bagoong (fermented, briny), garlic, and the green-vegetal scent of fresh vegetables. Texture: each vegetable should have its own texture — soft squash, creamy eggplant, firm-bitter melon, snappy beans. Feel: satisfying, complex, slightly challenging (the bitterness is not for everyone) — food that tastes like health and frugality.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Pinakbet lives or dies on the bitter melon. Without it, the dish loses its Ilocano identity — the bitterness is the point, not an ingredient to be tolerated. The bagoong quality is the second pivot: bagoong provides all the salt and seasoning, so thin or underfermented bagoong produces a flat dish. The liquid management is the third: pinakbet should not be watery — the vegetables must cook in their own steam, producing a concentrated, clinging sauce rather than a soupy broth. If water is added, the technique is broken.

## PH-13 — Bicol Express (Bicolano Chile-Coconut Technique)

**Cuisine:** Filipino — Bicolano / Bicol Region, Southeastern Luzon | Regional Technique | Difficulty: 2 | Time: 45–60 min

**Origin:**
Bicol Express is the Bicolano technique of braising protein (pork belly, shrimp, or mixed) in coconut cream with an aggressive quantity of chiles — it is the definitive expression of the Bicolano chile-heat doctrine. The name "Bicol Express" is modern (attributed to the restaurant Bicolano's Best in Malate, Manila, in the 1980s) but the technique it names — protein braised in gata (coconut cream) with siling labuyo (bird's-eye chiles) — is ancestral Bicolano cooking. Besa and Dorotan (Memories of Philippine Kitchens, 2006, ISBN 978-1584794516) document the Bicolano chile-heat doctrine: Bicol is the Philippines' heat centre, where chiles are not a seasoning but a structural flavour component used in quantities that would be excessive in other Filipino regional traditions. The coconut cream is not garnish — it is structural fat that carries the chile heat and provides the braising medium. The distinction from adobo sa gata (PH-2): Bicol express uses coconut cream as the primary braising medium from the start, not as a finishing enrichment; and the chile quantity is radically higher — dozens of chiles per pot, not 3–5.

**Description:**
The Bicolano method: slice pork belly into 2 cm strips (the fat is essential — lean pork does not work in this technique). In a heavy pot, combine pork belly, coconut cream (kakang gata — first press, thick), sliced onion, crushed garlic, sliced ginger, and bagoong alamang (PH-9, 2–3 tablespoons). Add chiles: 20–30 siling labuyo (bird's-eye chiles, sliced or left whole depending on desired heat intensity — sliced chiles release more capsaicin) or a combination of siling labuyo and siling mahaba (long green chiles, milder, providing bulk and green-chile flavour). Bring to a gentle boil. Reduce to a simmer. Cook uncovered for 30–45 min, stirring occasionally, until the coconut cream reduces, the pork fat renders, and the sauce thickens into a concentrated, oily, chile-flecked cream. The critical endpoint: the coconut cream should "break" — the coconut oil separates from the cream solids, producing a sauce that is simultaneously creamy and oily, with pools of chile-tinted coconut oil on the surface. The pork should be tender and infused with the coconut-chile-bagoong flavour.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Chile-as-structural-flavour thread: Bicolano chile doctrine connects to the global tradition of regional heat centres — Sichuan (ma la doctrine), Andhra Pradesh (India's heat centre), Bahia (Brazilian dendê-chile tradition), Yucatan (habanero country). In each case, the heat is not decorative but structural — the chile is as fundamental as salt. The coconut-cream-as-braising-medium thread connects to: Malaysian rendang, South Indian avial, Sri Lankan curries, and the Pacific lolo tradition (FJ-5). The bagoong-in-coconut-cream combination is distinctly Bicolano — the fermented paste adds an umami-funk layer beneath the heat and fat. → Related: PH-2, PH-9, FJ-5

**Flavour Context:**
The dominant flavour is heat-in-fat: the chiles provide capsaicin heat (50,000–100,000 Scoville from siling labuyo), and the coconut cream carries and distributes that heat in a lipid matrix, creating a sustained burn rather than a sharp spike. The bagoong adds salt-umami-funk beneath the heat. The pork fat, rendered during cooking, melds with the coconut oil to create a double-fat matrix. The overall profile: hot, creamy, savoury, funky, rich — Bicol Express is maximalist cooking, with every flavour dial turned up.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: fresh siling labuyo from Bicol markets (the Bicolano chiles are documented as hotter than those grown in other Philippine regions — Tayag, Linamnam, 2012), freshly pressed coconut cream, heritage pork belly, artisanal bagoong. Reserve: quality fresh chiles with good coconut cream and commercial bagoong. Market: dried chiles or chile flakes substituted for fresh — the heat character changes from bright and sharp to dark and smoky. House: Bicol Express made with store-bought coconut milk (thin) instead of cream (thick) is watery and lacks the fat-matrix that defines the dish.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the dish should be cream-yellow flecked with red and green chile pieces, with visible pools of coconut oil on the surface; the pork should be stained golden from the coconut cream. Aroma: coconut cream (sweet, fatty) layered with chile heat (capsaicin does not have a scent, but the chiles' vegetal aroma is present) and bagoong (fermented, briny). Texture: the sauce should be thick, creamy, and oily — not thin or brothy; the pork should be soft and yielding. Feel: the heat should build gradually (coconut fat delays capsaicin absorption, creating a slow burn that peaks after several bites), and the cream should coat the palate, making the heat feel embedded rather than superficial.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Bicol Express lives or dies on the chile quantity and the coconut cream quality. The chile quantity must be genuinely aggressive — 3–5 chiles is not Bicol Express, it is adobo sa gata with extra pepper. The Bicolano doctrine demands that the heat be central, not decorative. The coconut cream must be thick first-press (kakang gata) — thin coconut milk produces a watery sauce that cannot carry the heat properly. The bagoong must be present — without it, the dish is coconut-chile braise, not Bicol Express. The trifecta of coconut cream + aggressive chiles + bagoong is the identity of this dish.

## PH-14 — Sisig (Kapampangan Offal Transformation)

**Cuisine:** Filipino — Kapampangan / Pampanga, Central Luzon | Regional Technique | Difficulty: 3 | Time: 90–120 min

**Origin:**
Sisig is the Kapampangan technique of transforming pork offal (face, ears, jowl, brain) through successive heat applications — boil, grill, chop, sizzle — into a crispy, savoury, intensely flavoured dish served on a sizzling plate. The word "sisig" originally referred to a salad of green papaya and other fruits dressed in vinegar (documented by Fernandez, Palayok, 2000, ISBN 978-9715693776); the meat version was developed in the 1970s by Lucia Cunanan of Angeles City, Pampanga, who applied the acid-dressing technique to pork offal. Tayag (Linamnam, 2012, ISBN 978-9712726408) documents Cunanan's innovation and the dish's subsequent rise to national prominence. The technique is defined by its successive heat applications: (1) boiling — the pork face, ears, and jowl are boiled until tender, softening the cartilage and rendering the fat; (2) grilling — the boiled pieces are grilled over charcoal until charred, adding Maillard flavour and a smoky dimension; (3) chopping — the grilled pieces are chopped into small, uniform pieces on a wooden block; (4) sizzling — the chopped meat is tossed onto a sizzling-hot cast-iron plate with onion, chiles, calamansi, and — in the Cunanan original — chicken liver for creaminess. The sizzling plate is brought to the table with a raw egg cracked on top, which cooks in the residual heat. Sisig is Pampanga's most famous culinary export and the dish that brought Kapampangan cuisine to national and international attention.

**Description:**
The Kapampangan method: boil pork face (maskara), ears, and jowl in water with bay leaves, peppercorns, and salt until tender (45–60 min). Drain. Grill over charcoal until all surfaces are charred — the char is critical, providing smoky-bitter-caramelised notes. Chop the grilled pieces into 1 cm cubes on a wooden chopping block (the chopping is done with a heavy cleaver in a rhythmic, rapid motion that is itself a Kapampangan culinary performance). Heat a cast-iron sizzling plate until smoking. Add a small amount of oil or rendered pork fat. Add the chopped meat. Add minced onion, sliced siling labuyo, minced chicken liver (optional — the traditional Cunanan version includes it; modern versions often omit it). Season with calamansi juice, soy sauce, and pepper. Toss rapidly on the sizzling plate. Crack a raw egg on top. Serve immediately — the sizzle is part of the dish's identity. The diner mixes the egg into the hot meat, creating a creamy binding. The result: crispy, smoky, sour, creamy, with the gelatinous texture of rendered cartilage and the richness of pork fat.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Offal-transformation thread: sisig connects to the global tradition of elevating offal through technique — French tete de veau (calf's head, boiled and dressed), Mexican tacos de cabeza (head-meat tacos), Chinese pig-ear salad, Italian bollito misto. The successive-heat-application thread: the boil-grill-chop-sizzle sequence is distinctive — most offal preparations use a single cooking method; sisig's four-stage process is a technique compound. The sizzling-plate service connects to Korean dolsot (stone-pot service), Japanese teppanyaki (iron-plate cooking), and the broader Asian hot-plate tradition. Tayag notes that the sizzling plate was not Cunanan's original — early sisig was served on a banana leaf; the cast-iron plate was a later innovation that became inseparable from the dish. → Related: PH-5, PH-8, PH-19

**Flavour Context:**
The flavour is a four-layer compound: (1) boiled pork — gelatinous, fatty, neutral-savoury; (2) charcoal grill — smoky, bitter-caramelised, Maillard-complex; (3) acid dressing — calamansi-sour, onion-pungent, chile-hot; (4) sizzling plate — additional Maillard caramelisation, egg-creamy binding. The chicken liver, when present, adds a mineral, organ-meat richness beneath the surface flavours. The overall profile: crispy, smoky, sour, creamy, fatty, hot — every flavour dimension is activated. Sisig is maximalist Kapampangan cooking — nothing is subtle.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: sisig at a Pampanga specialist (Aling Lucing — Cunanan's original stall, now run by her descendants — or at Tayag's Bale Dutung), using whole pig face grilled over wood charcoal, chopped to order, served on a superheated sizzling plate with fresh egg. Reserve: sisig using proper offal cuts (face, ears, jowl — not generic ground pork) with charcoal grilling and sizzling-plate service. Market: sisig made with generic pork cuts (shoulder, belly) without the offal texture — edible but missing the gelatinous-cartilaginous character. House: ground-pork sisig without grilling or sizzling plate — this has become common in Manila chain restaurants but represents a technique degradation.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the sizzling plate should arrive at the table still sizzling — steam and fat spatters are part of the presentation; the meat should be dark from charring, flecked with white onion and green-red chiles; the raw egg on top should begin cooking on contact. Aroma: charcoal smoke, sizzling fat, calamansi citrus — the aroma should be aggressive, reaching diners at adjacent tables. Texture: the critical test — the meat should be crispy on the outside (from the sizzling plate) and gelatinous within (from the rendered cartilage and collagen); the egg, once mixed in, should create a creamy binding. Feel: intense, stimulating, designed to be eaten with cold beer — sisig is pulutan (drinking food) elevated to main-course status.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Sisig lives or dies on the successive heat applications. Skipping the grilling stage (boil-chop-sizzle) loses the smoky dimension that defines the dish. Using ground pork instead of chopped offal (face, ears, jowl) loses the textural complexity — the gelatinous cartilage, the crispy skin, the tender meat, all in a single bite. The sizzling plate must be genuinely hot — a warm plate does not produce the final caramelisation that creates the crispy exterior. The calamansi must be fresh-squeezed — bottled juice lacks the aromatic oils that make calamansi distinctive. The egg is optional but traditional — its absence changes the dish from creamy-crispy to merely crispy.

## PH-15 — Paksiw (Vinegar-Braising Technique Family)

**Cuisine:** Filipino — Pan-Philippine, strongest in Visayan tradition | Technique Family | Difficulty: 2 | Time: 30–60 min

**Origin:**
Paksiw is the Filipino vinegar-braising technique — protein (fish or pork) simmered in vinegar with aromatics until the acid permeates the flesh and the liquid reduces to a concentrated sauce. The word derives from "pasiw" (to cook in vinegar). Fernandez (Tikim, 1994) distinguishes paksiw from adobo: both use vinegar as a primary acid, but adobo includes soy sauce and reduces to a glaze, whereas paksiw retains more liquid and relies on vinegar as the dominant flavour. There are two primary branches: paksiw na isda (fish paksiw) and paksiw na lechon (pork paksiw using leftover lechon — see PH-17). The fish version is the older technique: whole fish (milkfish/bangus, tilapia, or grouper/lapu-lapu) braised in vinegar with ginger, garlic, onion, long green pepper (siling mahaba), and bitter melon or eggplant. The pork version: leftover lechon (whole roast pig) is re-braised in vinegar with liver sauce (lechon sarsa), garlic, and sugar — this is a second-day technique, transforming the dry leftovers of a feast into a new dish. Besa and Dorotan (Memories of Philippine Kitchens, 2006) note that paksiw is the Filipino cook's answer to the question of preservation: before refrigeration, vinegar-braising extended the life of cooked fish and meat by several days in tropical heat.

**Description:**
Paksiw na isda (fish): whole fish (cleaned, scored on both sides) placed in a wide pan. Add vinegar (coconut or cane, enough to half-submerge the fish), sliced ginger (generous — 5–6 slices), crushed garlic, sliced onion, whole siling mahaba (long green chiles), and salt. Optional: add sliced bitter melon or eggplant alongside the fish. Bring to a boil without stirring (same vinegar principle as adobo — do not disturb the acid during the initial heating). Simmer 15–20 min until the fish is cooked through and the liquid has reduced by half. Do not overcook — the fish should be firm and intact, not falling apart. Paksiw na lechon (pork): slice leftover lechon (PH-17) into serving pieces. Combine in a pot with vinegar, lechon liver sauce (a thick, dark sauce made from pureed pork liver, vinegar, sugar, and spices), garlic, bay leaves, and whole peppercorns. Add a small amount of sugar to balance the vinegar. Simmer 30–40 min until the lechon pieces have absorbed the vinegar and the sauce is thick and dark. The result: the crispy lechon skin softens and becomes gelatinous; the dry roast meat rehydrates in the vinegar sauce.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Vinegar-braising thread: paksiw connects to the global family of vinegar-braised dishes — Spanish escabeche (PH-21), Italian agrodolce, French sauce aigre-douce. The distinction: paksiw uses vinegar as the primary braising liquid (not a finishing sauce), making it more acid-dominant than most European vinegar preparations. The fish-in-vinegar thread connects to: Japanese nanbanzuke (fried fish marinated in vinegar), Filipino escabeche (PH-21 — a separate technique that uses sweet-sour sauce rather than vinegar braise), and Chinese vinegar fish (Hangzhou tradition). The second-day transformation thread (paksiw na lechon) connects to the global tradition of re-cooking feast leftovers: Mexican ropa vieja, British bubble and squeak, Italian ribollita. → Related: PH-1, PH-6, PH-17, PH-21

**Flavour Context:**
Paksiw na isda: the dominant flavour is sour-clean — the vinegar permeates the fish, and the ginger provides a warm aromatic counterpoint. The fish's own flavour is foregrounded by the simplicity of the preparation. The bitter melon, if included, adds the Ilocano-adjacent bitterness dimension. Paksiw na lechon: the dominant flavour is sour-rich — the vinegar's acidity cuts through the pork fat, and the liver sauce adds a dark, mineral, umami depth. The sugar provides a balancing sweetness. The overall profile (both versions): sour, clean, gingery (fish) or sour, rich, dark (pork).

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: paksiw na isda made with whole fresh fish from the morning market, native coconut vinegar, and fresh ginger — the fish quality is fully exposed by the simple preparation. Reserve: quality fish and vinegar; quality lechon leftovers for paksiw na lechon. Market: any fresh fish with standard vinegar — the technique is simple and forgiving. House: paksiw na lechon specifically requires actual lechon leftovers — it is a waste-not technique, not an independent preparation.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: paksiw na isda — whole fish in a clear, vinegar-based broth with visible ginger slices, green chiles, and optional vegetables; the fish should be intact, not broken. Paksiw na lechon — dark, thick sauce coating the pork pieces; the lechon skin should be visible but softened. Aroma: clean vinegar, ginger, garlic (fish version); dark liver sauce, vinegar, pork fat (lechon version). Texture: fish should be firm and flaky; lechon should be soft and gelatinous (the skin) with tender meat. Feel: refreshing (fish version — the acid and ginger are cleansing) or comforting (lechon version — the rich sauce and soft meat are satisfying).

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Paksiw na isda lives or dies on fish freshness — the simple vinegar braise exposes every flaw in the fish. Old fish produces a dish that tastes of ammonia and old oil. The ginger must be fresh — dried ginger powder is not a substitute. Paksiw na lechon lives or dies on the quality of the leftover lechon: dry, overcooked lechon remains dry even after re-braising; properly cooked lechon with good fat content rehydrates beautifully. The liver sauce (lechon sarsa) is essential — without it, paksiw na lechon is merely vinegar-braised pork.

## PH-16 — Sinigang (Tamarind-Acid Souring Technique)

**Cuisine:** Filipino — Pan-Philippine, strongest in Tagalog and Visayan traditions | Core Technique | Difficulty: 2 | Time: 45–60 min

**Origin:**
Sinigang is the Filipino souring technique — a broth-based preparation where the souring agent is the technique variable, not the protein. Fernandez (Tikim, 1994, ISBN 978-9712703836) identifies sinigang as one of the three foundational Filipino cooking techniques alongside adobo (PH-1) and kinilaw (PH-7): where adobo is vinegar-braising and kinilaw is acid-denaturation, sinigang is acid-in-broth — a sour soup that defines the Filipino meal alongside steaming rice. The canonical souring agent is sampalok (tamarind, Tamarindus indica) — fresh green tamarind pods boiled and strained to produce a sour, fruity broth. But the souring agent is the technique's variable: different regions and different cooks use different souring agents, each producing a distinct sinigang character. Documented souring agents include: sampalok (tamarind — the baseline), calamansi (citrus), kamias (Averrhoa bilimbi — a small, extremely sour fruit), guava, green mango, santol (Sandoricum koetjape), bayabas (guava), and even tomato. The protein is also variable: pork ribs (sinigang na baboy), shrimp (sinigang na hipon), fish (sinigang na isda — milkfish belly/bangus is preferred), or beef. The vegetable component is substantial: radish (labanos), kangkong (water spinach), string beans (sitaw), eggplant, okra, tomato, onion, taro leaves. Sinigang is a meal in itself — protein, vegetables, and sour broth served over rice.

**Description:**
The baseline method (tamarind sinigang): boil pork spare ribs or belly in water with sliced onion and tomato until the pork is tender (30–40 min). While the pork cooks, prepare the souring agent: boil 200–300 g fresh green tamarind pods in 2 cups water until soft (10–15 min); mash the softened pods through a strainer to extract the sour pulp; discard the seeds and fibres. Add the tamarind extract to the pork broth. Add vegetables in order of cooking time: radish and taro first, then string beans and eggplant, then kangkong (water spinach — added last, as it wilts within seconds). Season with patis (PH-10) and salt. Serve in the pot with steaming rice on the side. The critical principle: the broth must be genuinely sour — sinigang is not a mildly tangy soup but an assertively sour preparation where the acid opens the appetite and cuts through the richness of the pork or fish. The tamarind provides a fruity, rounded sourness distinct from vinegar's sharpness.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Sour-soup thread: sinigang connects to the Southeast Asian family of sour soups — Thai tom yum (sour-hot-aromatic, using lime and lemongrass), Vietnamese canh chua (tamarind sour soup — the closest structural parallel, also tamarind-based with fish and vegetables), Indonesian sayur asam (tamarind vegetable soup), Cambodian samlor machou (sour soup). The tamarind thread specifically connects sinigang to the Indian-origin tamarind corridor: tamarind (Tamarindus indica, native to East Africa, cultivated in India, spread to Southeast Asia) is the souring agent shared across this region. The Filipino distinction: sinigang uses tamarind as the sole flavouring acid (not combined with lemongrass, galangal, or kaffir lime as in Thai or Vietnamese versions), producing a cleaner, more direct sourness. → Related: PH-7, PH-10, PH-15

**Flavour Context:**
The dominant flavour is sour — the tamarind provides a fruity, rounded acidity that is softer and more complex than vinegar or citrus. The protein contributes savoury richness (pork fat, shrimp sweetness, or fish oil depending on the version). The vegetables add body and sweetness (radish, tomato) or green-vegetal notes (kangkong, string beans). The overall profile: sour, savoury, clean, refreshing — sinigang is appetite-stimulating food designed to be eaten with large quantities of rice. The sourness should dominate but not overwhelm — the broth should taste like sour fruit, not like acid.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: sinigang made with fresh green tamarind, strained by hand, using high-quality pork ribs or ultra-fresh shrimp, with market-fresh vegetables including kangkong and radish. Reserve: quality tamarind (fresh or frozen pulp) with good protein and fresh vegetables. Market: sinigang mix powder (Knorr Sinigang mix is the dominant commercial product) — functional and well-calibrated but the fresh tamarind's fruity complexity is replaced by citric-acid sharpness. House: commercial sinigang mix is ubiquitous in Filipino home cooking — it produces a recognisable sinigang but is to fresh tamarind as instant coffee is to freshly brewed.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: clear-to-slightly-cloudy sour broth (the tamarind adds a slight haze) with visible pork or shrimp, colourful vegetables (green kangkong, white radish, purple eggplant, red tomato), in a deep bowl. Aroma: sour-fruity (tamarind), savoury (pork broth or shrimp), green-vegetal (kangkong). Texture: the broth should be thin and clear, not thick or starchy; the pork should be tender; the vegetables should be cooked but not soft; the kangkong should be just-wilted, bright green. Feel: refreshing and appetite-stimulating — sinigang is the soup that makes you eat three cups of rice.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Sinigang lives or dies on the souring agent. Fresh tamarind produces a fruity, complex, rounded sourness that commercial mixes cannot replicate. The vegetable component must be substantial — sinigang without vegetables is just sour pork broth. The kangkong must be added at the last possible moment — overcooked kangkong is dark green and slimy; properly cooked kangkong is bright green and slightly crunchy. The patis (fish sauce) seasoning must be calibrated: too much patis adds a fishiness that competes with the tamarind's clean sourness; too little leaves the broth under-seasoned.
