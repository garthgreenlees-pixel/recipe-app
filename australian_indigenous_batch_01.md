## AI-1 — Quandong (Santalum acuminatum — Desert Peach)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — Anangu, Pitjantjatjara, Arrernte peoples / Central and Western Australia | Native Fruit | Difficulty: 1 (preparation) / 3 (sourcing wild) | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Quandong (Santalum acuminatum, family Santalaceae) is a native Australian fruit — a semi-parasitic tree of the arid and semi-arid inland that produces bright red, marble-sized fruit with a large, pitted stone. The Pitjantjatjara name is mangata; the Arrernte name is atnyematye. Quandong has been a staple food of Central and Western Desert peoples for at least 40,000 years — dried quandong has been found in archaeological deposits dated to the Pleistocene. The fruit is harvested in the wild from trees that grow as root parasites on other plants (most commonly acacia species). Quandong is both food and medicine: the fruit is eaten fresh or dried, the kernel is pressed for oil, and the wood and leaves have therapeutic uses in traditional medicine across multiple language groups. The species was one of the first Australian native foods to be commercially cultivated (from the 1970s onward), documented by Cherikoff in The Bushfood Handbook. Coulthard and Sullivan feature quandong in Warndu Mai (Hachette, 2019) as a primary Adnyamathanha food. Mindy Woods includes quandong in Karkalla at Home (Murdoch Books, 2024) as a versatile native fruit for contemporary cooking.

**Description:**
The fruit is approximately 15–25 mm in diameter, with thin, tart flesh surrounding a deeply pitted stone (the stone resembles a small brain). The flesh is bright red when ripe, turning darker as it dries. Fresh quandong flesh has a tart, acidic flavour with stone-fruit and rhubarb notes. Dried quandong (the traditional preservation method — the fruit is sun-dried in the desert climate and stored for months or years) has a more concentrated, leathery, apricot-like character. Preparation in traditional contexts: eaten fresh during the fruiting season (autumn to early winter); sun-dried and stored in bark containers or woven bags for lean seasons; the dried fruit is rehydrated in water before eating or cooking. Contemporary use: quandong is used in jams, chutneys, pies, sauces, and desserts. The kernel inside the stone contains an edible oil with a mild, nutty flavour — pressed for cooking oil or eaten roasted. Species: Santalum acuminatum (sweet quandong, the edible species). Santalum murrayanum (bitter quandong) has a different, more astringent flavour and is less commonly eaten.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Native-fruit thread: quandong connects to the broader global tradition of desert peoples preserving fruit by drying. The technique parallel is with Middle Eastern dried apricots, North African dried dates, and Central Asian dried mulberries — all desert fruits preserved by sun-drying for year-round use. The hemi-parasitic growth habit of quandong (Santalum) connects to the broader sandalwood family — Santalum album (Indian sandalwood) is a close relative, though not a food source. → Related: AI-2, AI-3, AI-4, AI-5

**Flavour Context:**
Fresh quandong: tart, acidic, with a complex flavour that combines stone fruit (peach, apricot), rhubarb, and a faint resinous note from the Santalaceae family. The acidity is pronounced — fresh quandong is rarely eaten on its own without sweetening. Dried quandong: more concentrated, sweeter, with a leathery, apricot-like character and a slightly smoky-resinous undertone. The kernel oil: mild, nutty, comparable to almond oil but with a faintly medicinal-herbal note. Quandong pairs with dairy (cream, yoghurt), honey, and other native fruits. In contemporary Australian cooking, quandong replaces stone fruit in European-style desserts (tarts, crumbles, compotes), bringing a distinctly Australian flavour to familiar forms.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: wild-harvested quandong from Central Australian desert country, picked at full ripeness, sun-dried on Country by Anangu or Arrernte harvesters. Reserve: sustainably wild-harvested quandong, commercially dried to food-safety standards. Market: orchard-grown quandong (now cultivated in South Australia and Western Australia) — consistent but less complex than wild fruit. House: pre-dried, pre-packaged quandong pieces from specialty retailers — acceptable for cooking but the full flavour is diminished.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: ripe fresh quandong is bright scarlet-red, glossy, approximately marble-sized. Dried quandong is dark red to brown, shrunken, leathery. The stone inside is distinctive — pale, hard, deeply pitted with a brain-like surface pattern. Aroma: fresh quandong has a tart, stone-fruit scent with a faint resinous note. Dried quandong has a concentrated, sweet-sour, apricot-like aroma. Texture: fresh flesh is thin (2–3 mm around the stone), slightly mealy, tart. Dried flesh is chewy and leathery. Feel: the tartness produces a mouth-puckering sensation similar to unripe plum.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Quandong lives or dies on ripeness and drying. Unripe fruit (green, hard) is excessively tart and astringent — inedible. Over-ripe fruit (soft, mushy, fermenting) develops off-flavours. The harvest window on the tree is approximately 2 weeks. Drying must occur in low humidity — the desert climate of Central Australia is the ideal drying environment. In humid coastal climates, quandong develops mould instead of drying cleanly.

## AI-2 — Kakadu Plum / Gubinge (Terminalia ferdinandiana — World's Highest Vitamin C Fruit)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — Jawoyn, Larrakia, Bardi, Jaru peoples / Northern Australia (Top End, Kimberley) | Native Fruit | Difficulty: 1 (preparation) / 3 (sourcing wild) | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Kakadu plum (Terminalia ferdinandiana, family Combretaceae) is a native Australian fruit from the woodlands and open forests of the Northern Territory and Kimberley region — a small, green, oval fruit approximately 2–3 cm long that holds the highest recorded concentration of vitamin C of any fruit on earth: up to 5,300 mg per 100g fresh weight (approximately 100 times the vitamin C of an orange). The Jawoyn name is murunga; the Bardi (Kimberley) name is gubinge. The fruit has been a food and medicine for Top End Aboriginal peoples for millennia. It is eaten fresh from the tree during the fruiting season (March–June), or dried and stored. The tree (a small deciduous tree, 4–10 metres tall) is found across the tropical savannas of the Top End. Kakadu plum has become one of the most commercially significant Australian native foods, used in the cosmetics industry for its antioxidant properties and in the food industry for preserves, sauces, and freeze-dried powder. Woods features gubinge in Karkalla at Home (2024). CSIRO has documented the species extensively through the Australian National Herbarium and published research on its phytochemical properties.

**Description:**
The fruit is small (2–3 cm long, approximately 10g), oval, with smooth green skin that may develop a slight yellow tinge when fully ripe. The flesh is pale green to cream, surrounding a single, hard seed. Fresh Kakadu plum has a tart, astringent, sour-green flavour — intensely acidic from the extreme vitamin C concentration, with notes of stewed apple and pear. The astringency comes from tannins and ellagic acid. Eaten fresh: the fruit is picked from the tree or gathered from the ground (ripe fruit falls) and eaten whole — the skin, flesh, and sometimes the seed (cracked for the kernel). Dried: the fruit is sun-dried and stored for off-season use. Contemporary use: freeze-dried Kakadu plum powder is used as a preservative (the vitamin C and ellagic acid inhibit oxidation and bacterial growth), a flavouring agent in sauces, jams, and desserts, and a nutritional supplement. The powder is added to everything from ice cream to gin.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Superfoods-and-preservation thread: Kakadu plum's extreme vitamin C content connects to the global interest in high-antioxidant native fruits — acai (Brazil), maqui berry (Chile), goji berry (China). But unlike those fruits, Kakadu plum has a genuine deep-time food history — it has been eaten by Aboriginal peoples for millennia, not rediscovered by modern wellness culture. Its use as a natural preservative (the high acid content inhibits bacterial growth) parallels the use of citrus in European preservation and lime in Pacific raw-fish preparations. → Related: AI-1, AI-3, AI-4, AI-5

**Flavour Context:**
The dominant flavour is tart — intensely, almost aggressively sour. The tartness is followed by astringency (tannins, ellagic acid) that tightens the mouth. Behind the acid, there are notes of stewed apple, pear, and a faintly herbaceous-green character. The flavour softens when the fruit is cooked or processed: jams and sauces made from Kakadu plum have a more balanced sweet-tart profile. The freeze-dried powder retains the tartness but concentrates it — a small amount (1–2 g) is sufficient to add pronounced sourness to a dish. Species: Terminalia ferdinandiana is the edible species. Other Australian Terminalia species exist but are not food sources.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: fresh Kakadu plum eaten from the tree in season on Country in the Top End — the full vitamin C content is at its peak in fresh fruit and degrades with storage. Reserve: sustainably wild-harvested fruit, processed into powder or paste within 48 hours of harvest by Indigenous-owned enterprises. Market: commercially produced Kakadu plum powder from certified suppliers. House: blended products where Kakadu plum is a minor ingredient, often used as a marketing claim rather than a meaningful flavour contribution.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the fresh fruit is pale green, oval, and unassuming — it does not look like a superfood. The flesh is pale cream-green. Aroma: a mild, green, slightly apple-like scent — not strongly aromatic. Texture: the flesh is firm and fibrous, with a mealy quality when fully ripe. The skin is thin. Feel: the vitamin C produces an immediate, sharp sour sensation on the tongue, followed by astringent tightening of the cheeks and gums. The experience is more sour than any commercially available fruit.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Kakadu plum lives or dies on freshness. The vitamin C content degrades with time, heat, and light — fresh fruit contains up to 5,300 mg/100g; improperly stored or processed fruit may contain a fraction of that. For culinary use, the fruit or powder must be added late in cooking (or used raw) to preserve its acid character. Extended cooking breaks down the vitamin C and flattens the flavour. For the native food industry, the critical quality control is the processing chain: from tree to processing within 48 hours, freeze-dried (not heat-dried), stored in opaque, airtight containers.

## AI-3 — Davidson's Plum (Davidsonia spp. — Subtropical Rainforest Fruit)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — Bundjalung, Githabul peoples / Subtropical rainforest, Northern NSW and North Queensland | Native Fruit | Difficulty: 1 (preparation) | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Davidson's plum refers to three species of native Australian rainforest fruit in the genus Davidsonia (family Cunoniaceae): D. jerseyana (northern NSW), D. pruriens (north Queensland), and D. johnsonii (smooth Davidson's plum, northeast NSW). The fruit is a dark burgundy-purple plum-shaped drupe, 4–6 cm long, with intensely sour, astringent flesh and a deep purple-red juice that stains everything it touches. The fruit grows in subtropical and tropical rainforests — Country of the Bundjalung and Githabul peoples in northern NSW and the Djabugay and Yidinji peoples in the Atherton Tablelands of north Queensland. Davidson's plum has been eaten by Aboriginal peoples of these regions as a seasonal fruit, gathered from the forest floor (the ripe fruit falls from the tree). Woods (Bundjalung) features Davidson's plum prominently in Karkalla at Home (2024) as a signature ingredient of her Bundjalung Country. The fruit is now commercially cultivated in subtropical Australia and is used in jams, sauces, wines, and as a natural colouring agent.

**Description:**
The fruit is egg-shaped, 4–6 cm long, with thin, dark purple-blue skin and deep purple-crimson flesh. Fresh from the tree, the fruit is intensely sour and astringent — too tart for most people to eat raw. The traditional preparation: eaten fresh (sparingly) or mixed with other foods to balance the acidity. The juice is extracted by squeezing or boiling the fruit. Contemporary preparation: Davidson's plum is cooked with sugar to produce jams, sauces, and syrups. It is used in desserts (cakes, ice cream, pannacotta), in savoury sauces for game meats (kangaroo, emu, duck), and in cocktails. The intense colour makes it a natural food colouring — a few tablespoons of Davidson's plum puree turns a cream or mousse deep purple. The three species have slightly different flavour profiles: D. jerseyana is the most common commercially; D. pruriens is the largest fruited; D. johnsonii is rare and not commonly cultivated.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Native-sour-fruit thread: Davidson's plum connects to the global culinary tradition of using intensely sour native fruits in cooking — similar in function to Japanese umeboshi (pickled ume), Mexican tejocote, or European sloe (Prunus spinosa). The high anthocyanin content (responsible for the deep purple colour) connects to the global interest in antioxidant-rich fruits, but the primary culinary value is the acidity and colour. In contemporary Australian cuisine, Davidson's plum occupies the role that blackcurrant occupies in British and French cooking — an intensely flavoured, deeply coloured berry used in sauces, preserves, and desserts. → Related: AI-1, AI-2, AI-4, AI-5

**Flavour Context:**
The dominant flavour is sour — sharp, astringent, with a complex stone-fruit acidity that combines elements of plum, blackberry, and rhubarb. The astringency is pronounced (high tannin content) and produces a mouth-drying sensation. Cooked with sugar, the sourness becomes a tart-sweet balance — the resulting jam or sauce has a flavour profile comparable to blackcurrant jam but with a more complex, tropical-fruit undertone. The deep purple juice has a flavour that persists on the palate — it is not a subtle ingredient. Species: Davidsonia jerseyana (the commercially grown species) has fruit 4–5 cm long; D. pruriens (Queensland Davidson's plum) has larger fruit, up to 6 cm.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: wild-harvested Davidson's plum from Bundjalung Country rainforest, gathered from the forest floor at full ripeness. Reserve: orchard-grown fruit from subtropical NSW farms, picked at optimal ripeness (dark purple, slightly soft). Market: commercially produced Davidson's plum puree, paste, or frozen fruit. House: dried Davidson's plum powder — the flavour is concentrated but the fresh fruit character is diminished.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the ripe fruit is dark burgundy-purple, almost black, with a matte skin. Cut open, the flesh is deep crimson-purple. The juice stains hands, cutting boards, and cloth a vivid purple that is resistant to washing. Aroma: a mildly fruity, plum-like scent with a faint herbaceous note — not strongly aromatic until cooked. Texture: the flesh is soft and pulpy when ripe, with a thin skin and a single seed. Feel: the astringency produces an immediate tightening of the mouth — tannins are high.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Davidson's plum lives or dies on the sugar balance. The fruit is too sour and astringent to use without sweetening in most applications. Too little sugar: the sourness overwhelms everything else. Too much sugar: the distinctive tart character is lost and the result is generic "sweet purple sauce." The correct balance preserves the sour-tart identity while making the fruit palatable. The ratio varies by application — jams require more sugar (approximately 1:1 fruit to sugar); sauces for game meat require less (the sourness is intentional, cutting through rich fat).

## AI-4 — Finger Lime (Citrus australasica — Caviar Lime)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — Bundjalung, Githabul, Jagunna peoples / Subtropical rainforest, Northern NSW and Southeast Queensland | Native Citrus | Difficulty: 1 (preparation) | Time: minimal

**Origin:**
Finger lime (Citrus australasica, family Rutaceae) is a native Australian citrus species from the subtropical rainforests of the NSW-Queensland border region — Country of the Bundjalung and related peoples. The fruit is cylindrical, 6–12 cm long and 1–2 cm in diameter, resembling a finger (hence the common name). When cut open, the juice vesicles inside are not fused into segments (as in conventional citrus) but separate into individual pearl-like spheres that burst on the tongue, releasing a sharp, lime-like juice. This characteristic has earned the fruit the marketing name "citrus caviar." The finger lime is one of six native Australian Citrus species (C. australasica, C. australis, C. glauca, C. inodora, C. garrawayi, C. gracilis) — Australia is one of the ancestral homelands of the Citrus genus, which is significant given the global importance of citrus in cooking. Aboriginal peoples of the region have eaten finger limes for millennia, gathering the fruit from understorey trees in the rainforest. Woods features finger lime in Karkalla at Home (2024). The fruit has become internationally significant in haute cuisine, exported to restaurants in Tokyo, New York, London, and Paris.

**Description:**
The fruit is cylindrical, 6–12 cm long, approximately finger-width. The skin is thin and may be green, pink, red, yellow, or purple depending on the cultivar. When the fruit is cut in half crosswise, the individual juice vesicles (pulp pearls) spill out — these are separate, round, 2–3 mm in diameter, and firm enough to pick up individually. When bitten, each pearl bursts, releasing a sharp, acidic lime juice. This burst-on-the-tongue characteristic is the defining feature and the reason for the "citrus caviar" comparison. The fruit is used raw — cooking destroys the pearl structure and releases all the juice at once, negating the point. In contemporary cuisine: finger lime pearls are placed on top of raw fish, oysters, sashimi, desserts, cocktails, and anywhere that a burst of fresh acid is desired. The colour of the pearls varies by cultivar: pale green (most common), pink (from red-skinned cultivars), champagne (from yellow cultivars). Species: Citrus australasica is the finger lime. C. australis (round lime) and C. glauca (desert lime) are related native citrus species with different characteristics.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Native-citrus thread: the finger lime is Australia's contribution to the global citrus family — a reminder that Citrus has Australian origins as well as Southeast Asian ones. The molecular and textural novelty (individual juice pearls) has no parallel in any other citrus species worldwide. The culinary function — a sharp acid garnish — places finger lime in the same role as yuzu in Japanese cooking, calamansi in Filipino cooking, and lime in Pacific raw-fish preparations. The connection to the Pacific raw-fish thread (FJ-2, WS-2, TP-1) is functional rather than historical: finger lime performs the same acid function as lime juice, but with a textural element (the pop of the pearls) that lime juice cannot provide. → Related: AI-1, AI-2, AI-3, AI-5

**Flavour Context:**
The flavour is sharp, clean, intensely citric — pure lime acid without the bitterness of lime pith or the sweetness of lime zest. The acid is immediate and bright, but short-lived (the small volume of juice in each pearl means the acid burst is brief). Behind the acid, there is a faint herbaceous, slightly floral note that distinguishes finger lime from Persian or Key lime. The pink-pearled cultivars have a very faint berry-like undertone. The overall flavour impact is about texture as much as taste — the pop of each pearl, the burst of acid, the clean finish. Finger lime is an accent, not a base — it is used in small quantities (a teaspoon or less) to provide sharp punctuation.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: fresh finger lime from a rainforest-edge tree on Bundjalung Country, picked at full ripeness (the fruit yields slightly when pressed and the pearls are fully formed and firm). Reserve: orchard-grown fruit from a subtropical farm, picked in season (December to May). Market: fresh finger lime from specialty greengrocers or farmer's markets. House: frozen finger lime pearls (commercially available) — the pearls retain their shape after freezing and thawing, making this one of the few native fruits that freezes well.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: cut open, the pearls should be separate, round, and firm — they should tumble out of the fruit like tiny beads. If the pearls are soft, mushy, or fused together, the fruit is over-ripe or has been mishandled. Aroma: a clean, bright, lime-citrus scent with no bitterness. Texture: the defining test — each pearl should pop distinctly between the teeth, releasing a burst of juice. If the pearls are soft and do not pop, they are past their prime. Feel: the acid burst on the tongue should be sharp, clean, and immediate.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Finger lime lives or dies on freshness and temperature. The pearls must be firm and intact — old, dehydrated, or bruised fruit produces pearls that are limp and do not burst. The fruit must be served raw and cold — heat dissolves the pectin walls of the juice vesicles, collapsing the pearls into ordinary lime juice (at which point you might as well use a regular lime). The second pivot: quantity. Finger lime is an accent — too many pearls overwhelm a dish with acid. The correct amount is a teaspoon or less per serving, placed precisely on top of the food so the diner encounters individual bursts of acid with each bite.

## AI-5 — Bush Tomato / Akudjura (Solanum centrale — Desert Solanaceae)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — Arrernte, Alyawarre, Warlpiri peoples / Central Australian Desert | Native Fruit / Spice | Difficulty: 1 (preparation) | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Bush tomato (Solanum centrale, family Solanaceae) is a small, sun-dried fruit from the Central Australian desert — Country of the Arrernte, Alyawarre, and Warlpiri peoples. The Arrernte name is akudjura (also akatjura). The fruit grows on a low, sprawling shrub in sandy desert soils, producing small (1–2 cm diameter), round, yellow-green berries that ripen to dark brown-black. The fresh fruit is not commonly eaten — it is mildly toxic when green (solanine, a glycoalkaloid common to the Solanaceae family). The fruit is harvested when it has dried on the bush in the desert sun, at which point the solanine has degraded to safe levels and the fruit has concentrated into a chewy, intensely flavoured dried berry with a complex spice-like character. Bush tomato has been a staple food of Central Desert peoples for millennia — dried and stored in large quantities for lean seasons. The flavour profile is so complex that bush tomato functions as a spice rather than a fruit in contemporary cooking. Coulthard and Sullivan feature bush tomato prominently in Warndu Mai (2019) as one of the most versatile native ingredients. Woods includes it in Karkalla at Home (2024).

**Description:**
The dried fruit is approximately 1–2 cm in diameter, wrinkled, dark brown to black, resembling a small dried raisin or a whole dried cranberry. The texture is chewy-leathery. The flavour is extraordinary in its complexity: sun-dried tomato, caramel, tamarind, savoury umami, and a faint peppery heat. This flavour density is concentrated by the desert drying process — the fruit dehydrates on the bush over weeks, concentrating sugars, acids, and flavour compounds. In traditional contexts: the dried berries are eaten as a snack, ground into a paste, or mixed with water to make a savoury drink. In contemporary cooking: bush tomato is used as a spice (ground into powder and added to rubs, sauces, and seasonings), as a chutney ingredient, in bread dough, in sauces for kangaroo and other game meats, and as a flavour concentrate in soups and stews. The ground powder is sold commercially as "akudjura" by native food suppliers. Safety: only the fully dried fruit (Solanum centrale) is edible. Other Australian Solanum species are toxic — correct species identification is critical.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Dried-fruit-as-spice thread: bush tomato occupies the same functional space as sun-dried tomato in Italian cooking, tamarind in South and Southeast Asian cooking, and dried barberry in Persian cooking — an intensely flavoured dried fruit used as a savoury seasoning rather than a dessert ingredient. The Solanaceae family connection links bush tomato to the global tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), potato (S. tuberosum), and chilli (Capsicum spp.) — all New World species that were domesticated from wild Solanaceae. Australia's Solanum centrale is an independent development within the same family, never domesticated but managed through fire-regime practices that encouraged new growth. → Related: AI-1, AI-2, AI-3, AI-4

**Flavour Context:**
The flavour is the most complex of any Australian native fruit: a layered combination of sun-dried tomato (deep, savoury, umami), caramel (concentrated natural sugars), tamarind (sour-sweet, fruity acidity), and a faint peppery or smoky undertone. The flavour is savoury-dominant — bush tomato reads as a spice or condiment, not as a fruit. Ground bush tomato powder added to a kangaroo rub produces a distinctively Australian flavour profile that cannot be replicated with any non-native ingredient. The umami character comes from natural glutamate compounds concentrated by drying. Key pairing: bush tomato + kangaroo + wattleseed (AI-6) is the canonical "Australian native" flavour trio in contemporary cooking.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: wild-harvested dried bush tomato from Central Australian desert country, gathered from the bush at the correct dryness by Arrernte or Alyawarre harvesters. The desert sun-drying process produces the best flavour concentration. Reserve: wild-harvested, commercially processed and packaged by Indigenous-owned enterprises. Market: cultivated bush tomato, commercially dried — consistent quality but potentially less complex than wild fruit. House: pre-ground bush tomato powder from specialty retailers — convenient but loses freshness quickly once ground (volatile flavour compounds degrade with exposure to air).

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the dried fruit should be dark brown-black, wrinkled, and leathery — resembling a dried raisin. Avoid any fruit with green patches (may indicate unripe fruit with residual solanine). Aroma: complex — sun-dried tomato, caramel, a faint peppery-smoky note. Ground powder should have a strong, immediate aroma; if it smells flat or dusty, it is stale. Texture: the whole dried fruit should be chewy and leathery, not hard or brittle (brittle indicates over-drying or age). Feel: chewing a dried bush tomato produces an immediate umami-savoury flavour that builds and lingers — the flavour persists on the palate for minutes.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Bush tomato lives or dies on correct species identification and drying state. Only Solanum centrale is edible when dried. Other Australian Solanum species (S. chippendalei, S. cleistogamum, S. ellipticum) range from mildly irritating to toxic. In commercial settings, species identification is controlled by the supplier, but wild harvesters must be experienced in species recognition. The second pivot: the drying state. The fruit must be fully dried on the bush (not picked green and artificially dried) to ensure solanine degradation. Artificially dried or incompletely dried fruit may retain enough solanine to cause nausea and digestive distress.
