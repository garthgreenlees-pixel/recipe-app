## NZ-1 — The Hāngi (Māori Earth Oven)

**Cuisine:** Māori | Earth-Oven Cookery | Difficulty: 3 | Time: 4–8 hours

**Origin:**
The hāngi is the Māori earth oven — the final expression of the Austronesian earth-oven thread that runs unbroken from Taiwan to Aotearoa. Stones are heated in a fire pit, food is placed on the stones in woven flax (harakeke) baskets, covered with wet cloth and earth, and left to steam-cook for three to eight hours. The technique arrived with the Polynesian settlers who reached Aotearoa around 1280 CE, carrying the method from the Samoan umu and the Fijian lovo. In the colder climate of Aotearoa, the hāngi adapted: no banana leaves (too cold to grow), so harakeke flax and damp cloth replaced them. No breadfruit or taro (too cold), so kūmara (sweet potato), root vegetables, and meat — pork, chicken, mutton, and later lamb and beef — became the pit's contents. The hāngi is cooked for hui (gatherings), tangihanga (funerals), and celebrations. Every iwi has its own hāngi style: some use mānuka wood exclusively; some wrap food in cabbage leaves; some cook seafood separately from meat to preserve flavour integrity.

**Description:**
A pit is dug roughly 1 metre deep. Hardwood — ideally mānuka (Leptospermum scoparium) — is burned down to coals. Volcanic stones or dense river stones are heated in the fire until white-hot (approximately 800°C / 1,470°F at the stone surface). Food is layered in woven harakeke baskets: meat on the bottom (closest to the stones), root vegetables above, lighter items on top. Wet cloth — traditionally soaked in water, sometimes in seawater for coastal hāngi — covers the baskets. Earth is shovelled over the top to seal the pit. The sealed oven steams for four to eight hours depending on volume. The result is food with a distinctive smoky-earthy flavour that no oven or smoker replicates — the taste of the ground itself entering the food.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
The earth-oven thread: TW-2 (Taiwanese stone-boiling) → Indonesian barapen → FJ-1 (Fijian lovo) → Samoan/Tongan umu → HI-1 (Hawaiian imu) → NZ-1 (hāngi). The hāngi is the terminal node. It is the southernmost, coldest expression of the earth oven on the Pacific Migration Trail. What changes along the thread: the wrapping material (banana leaf → ti leaf → harakeke flax), the starch (taro → breadfruit → kūmara), the protein (pork throughout, but lamb enters in Aotearoa from European farming). What remains constant: heated stones, a sealed pit, long slow cooking, communal feasting.

**Flavour Context:**
The hāngi produces a flavour no other cooking method creates: smoky from the wood (mānuka smoke has a warm, resinous character distinct from European hardwoods), mineral from the stones, earthy from the ground, and sweet from the slow caramelisation of kūmara and root vegetables. Pork belly in the hāngi renders completely — the fat bastes the meat over hours. Chicken becomes fall-apart tender with a smoky skin. Kūmara caramelises into a dense, sweet mass. Stuffing (a Pākehā addition to the Māori method) absorbs all the steam flavours. Watercress or pūhā (Sonchus oleraceus, sow thistle) is sometimes added as a bitter green counterpoint.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: A marae hāngi where the pit master has decades of experience, the stones are volcanic basalt, the wood is dry mānuka, and the cooking time is calibrated to the load. The earth itself contributes flavour. GOOD: A well-executed hāngi at a family gathering with proper stone selection and timing. ADEQUATE: A commercially prepared hāngi using shortcuts (gas-heated stones, reduced cooking time). INSUFFICIENT: An oven-baked "hāngi" using liquid smoke — this is not a hāngi; it is roast meat with flavouring.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: when the earth is peeled back, steam rises in a dense white column; the cloth is brown with rendered fat and earth. Aroma: the moment the pit opens — mānuka smoke, rendered pork fat, caramelised kūmara, wet earth, mineral stone. This smell is the hāngi; there is no equivalent. Texture: meat should pull apart with no resistance; kūmara should be dense and caramelised, not watery. Feel: the stones, when checked before loading, should hiss and spit when water is flicked onto them — if they don't, the fire was not hot enough.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
The stones. If the stones are not hot enough, the hāngi steams weakly and the food boils rather than roasts. If the pit is not sealed properly, heat escapes and the cooking time doubles. If the wrong stones are used (porous sedimentary stones instead of dense volcanic basalt), the stones can crack or explode. The pit master reads the stones the way a sushi chef reads the fish — by decades of pattern recognition. The hāngi lives or dies at the moment the stones are declared ready.

**Doors:**
→ Earth-oven thread terminal: TW-2 → ID barapen → FJ-1 Lovo → WS umu → HI-1 Imu → NZ-1 Hāngi. The longest cooking-method thread on the Pacific Migration Trail. → HI-1, FJ-1, WS-umu
→ Mānuka smoke connects to NZ-20 Mānuka and parallels kiawe smoke in Hawaiʻi. → NZ-20, HI-8

## NZ-2 — Kaimoana (Māori Seafood Traditions)

**Cuisine:** Māori (coastal iwi) | Seafood Gathering & Preparation | Difficulty: varies | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Kaimoana (kai = food, moana = sea) is the Māori term for seafood and the cultural practices surrounding its gathering. For coastal iwi, kaimoana was — and remains — the primary protein source. The practice connects directly to the raw-fish thread of the Pacific Migration Trail: TW-1 Silaw → PH-1 Kinilaw → FJ-2 Kokoda → WS-2 Oka → HI-4 Poke → NZ-2 Kaimoana. But where every other stop on the trail developed elaborate preparations (acids, coconut cream, spices), Māori seafood traditions strip back to almost nothing. Kina (sea urchin) eaten raw from the shell on the rocks. Pāua (abalone, Haliotis iris) prised from rocks at low tide and eaten raw or lightly pounded and fried. Crayfish (Jasus edwardsii) boiled in seawater. The cold, nutrient-rich waters of Aotearoa produce seafood of such quality that heavy preparation would mask it.

**Description:**
Kaimoana encompasses: kina (Evechinus chloroticus, NZ sea urchin) — roe eaten raw; pāua (Haliotis iris, NZ abalone) — the foot eaten raw, pounded, or minced; kūtai (Perna canaliculus, green-lipped mussels) — steamed or fermented as toroi; pipi and tuatua (surf clams) — steamed or eaten raw; crayfish (Jasus edwardsii) — boiled or grilled; snapper/tāmure (Chrysophrys auratus) — the North Island's premier eating fish; kahawai (Arripis trutta) — smoked over mānuka. Gathering is governed by tikanga (cultural protocol) and rāhui (temporary closures of fishing grounds to allow stocks to recover) — a conservation practice that predates Western fisheries management by centuries.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Raw-fish thread terminal: the Māori approach to raw seafood is the most minimal on the trail. Silaw (Taiwan) uses salt. Kinilaw (Philippines) uses vinegar. Kokoda (Fiji) and Oka (Samoa) use coconut cream and citrus. Poke (Hawaiʻi) uses soy, sesame, kukui nut. Kaimoana uses almost nothing — because the seafood quality, straight from cold Southern Ocean waters, needs nothing. The thread simplifies as it moves south — from complex marinade to no marinade at all.

**Flavour Context:**
NZ's cold waters (10–18°C / 50–64°F surface temperature) produce seafood with firm texture and clean, mineral flavour. Kina roe is sweet, briny, creamy — one of the most intensely marine flavours on earth. Pāua has a firm, almost crunchy texture raw; tenderised, it becomes silky. Crayfish from the Kaikōura coast tastes of clean ocean and kelp. The minimal preparation philosophy: if the seafood needs sauce, the seafood is not good enough.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Kina cracked open on the rocks minutes after gathering, eaten with fingers. Pāua prised from rocks at a minus tide, sliced thin, eaten raw. Crayfish pulled from a pot in Kaikōura, split and eaten with lemon. GOOD: Same-day caught, prepared simply at home. ADEQUATE: Restaurant-quality kaimoana, well-sourced. INSUFFICIENT: Frozen, imported, or overcooked — this is no longer kaimoana; it is just fish.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: kina — the roe should be bright orange-gold, firm, with no grey discolouration. Pāua — the foot should be firm and dark with an iridescent shell. Aroma: raw kaimoana should smell of clean ocean, kelp, and mineral — never of ammonia or old fish. Texture: kina roe should be creamy but hold its shape on the tongue; pāua raw should have a firm snap.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Freshness. Kaimoana is gathered and consumed — the time between ocean and mouth is the only variable that matters. Every hour of delay loses flavour. The Māori practice of eating on the rocks, at the water's edge, is not romantic tradition; it is the technically optimal method. The dish lives at the moment of gathering. It dies in the chiller.

**Doors:**
→ Raw-fish thread terminal: TW-1 Silaw → PH-1 Kinilaw → FJ-2 Kokoda → WS-2 Oka → HI-4 Poke → NZ-2 Kaimoana. → HI-4, FJ-2, WS-2, TW-1, PH-1
→ Kina connects to uni (Japanese sea urchin). Same animal class, same preparation philosophy (raw, minimal), different species. → JP-[uni]
→ Pāua connects to HI-79 ʻOpihi and NZ-11 Pāua (species-specific entry). → HI-79, NZ-11

## NZ-3 — Kūmara (The Crop That Replaced the Mother)

**Cuisine:** Māori / NZ | Root Vegetable | Difficulty: 1 | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Kūmara (Ipomoea batatas, sweet potato) is the crop that made Māori settlement possible. The Polynesian settlers who reached Aotearoa around 1280 CE carried taro (the mother crop of Polynesia, the staple from Samoa to Hawaiʻi). But taro will not grow in Aotearoa — the climate is too cold. Kūmara, which tolerates cooler temperatures, survived. It became the crop of Rongo — the Māori god of cultivated food and peace. Elaborate kūmara storage pits (rua kūmara) were engineered into hillsides with ventilation channels to regulate temperature and humidity. The kūmara harvest cycle governed the Māori agricultural calendar. Where taro dies, kūmara steps in — and the thread of Polynesian starch-cultivation continues in a different form.

**Description:**
Three main cultivars in modern NZ: Owairaka Red (purple-red skin, orange flesh, sweet), Beauregard (copper skin, deep orange flesh, very sweet — an American import now dominant), and gold kūmara (pale yellow, milder). The Māori cultivar — the original Polynesian-carried variety — was smaller, with white flesh and a more starchy, less sweet character than modern commercial kūmara. Kūmara is roasted in the hāngi (where it caramelises over hours), baked, boiled and mashed, made into chips, or used in soups. Hāngi kūmara is the benchmark: the long, slow steam-roasting converts starch to sugar, producing a dense, sweet, smoky result.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Taro thread: TW-4 Cinavu (millet replaces taro in Taiwan) → PH/ID taro dishes → FJ-3 Rourou (taro leaves) → WS-1 Palusami (taro leaves in coconut cream) → HI-3 Poi / HI-5 Laulau (taro root and leaves) → NZ-3 Kūmara (taro dies, sweet potato replaces). The thread documents the most dramatic crop substitution on the trail. The cultural importance transfers: just as taro is sacred in Hawaiʻi (the elder brother Hāloa), kūmara is sacred in Aotearoa (the crop of Rongo). The spiritual role of the starch crop is constant; only the species changes.

**Flavour Context:**
Hāngi kūmara: dense, sweet, smoky, with caramelised edges. Roasted kūmara: sweet, tender, with crisp edges when done at high heat (220°C / 430°F). Mashed kūmara: creamy, sweet, pairs with butter and a pinch of NZ sea salt. Kūmara chips: crispy exterior, sweet interior. Gold kūmara is milder and works as a neutral starch; Owairaka Red is sweeter and better for roasting; Beauregard is the sweetest and best for mashing.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Hāngi kūmara — cooked for 5+ hours in the earth oven. Dense, sweet, smoky, with the flavour of the ground. GOOD: Oven-roasted Owairaka Red at high heat — caramelised edges, sweet interior. ADEQUATE: Boiled or steamed kūmara. INSUFFICIENT: Microwaved — the rapid heating produces a watery, textureless result.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: hāngi kūmara should have darkened, caramelised skin and dense, deep-orange flesh. Roasted kūmara should have visibly caramelised edges. Aroma: roasting kūmara smells sweet, slightly nutty; hāngi kūmara carries the mānuka smoke. Texture: properly cooked kūmara is dense and creamy, never watery. If it's watery, it was cooked too fast at too low a temperature.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Time and heat. Kūmara needs slow, sustained heat to convert its starch to sugar. The hāngi is the perfect method because the temperature is moderate (around 100°C / 212°F in the steam chamber) but sustained for hours. Fast, high heat caramelises the outside but leaves the interior starchy. The kūmara lives or dies at the starch-to-sugar conversion — which is a function of time, not temperature.

**Doors:**
→ Taro-to-kūmara crop substitution is the most dramatic adaptation on the Pacific Migration Trail. → HI-3 Poi, WS-1 Palusami, FJ-3 Rourou
→ Kūmara is the crop of Rongo (Māori god of cultivated food). Rongomaraeroa at Te Papa is named for the same deity. → NZ-42b
→ Hawaiian ʻuala (sweet potato, HI-32) is the same species — Ipomoea batatas — carried by the same migration. → HI-32

## NZ-4 — Horopito (NZ Pepper Leaf)

**Cuisine:** Māori | Native Spice | Difficulty: 1 | Time: varies

**Origin:**
Horopito (Pseudowintera colorata, NZ pepper tree, also called bush pepper or mountain horopito) is the most important native spice in Māori cuisine. The leaves contain polygodial — the same compound found in Tasmanian pepper (Tasmannia lanceolata, Australia) and water pepper (Persicaria hydropiper, global). The flavour is hot, peppery, slightly numbing, with a bitter-medicinal finish. Horopito has been used in rongoā Māori (traditional medicine) for centuries — anti-fungal, anti-microbial, used for toothache, skin conditions, and stomach ailments. Monique Fiso brought horopito into the fine-dining context at Hiakai, using it as a seasoning, a crust for fish and meat, and an infusion for syrups and oils. OCHO Chocolate in Dunedin produces a horopito and kawakawa chocolate bar (66% cacao Papua New Guinea chocolate) — a Pacific Migration Trail ingredient meeting a Pacific-sourced cacao in a Dunedin factory.

**Description:**
Horopito leaves are harvested from the bush — the plant grows wild throughout NZ's native forests, particularly at altitude (it thrives in sub-alpine conditions). The fresh leaves are intensely peppery; dried and ground, they mellow slightly but retain their heat. Horopito is used as: a pepper substitute (ground leaves), a crust ingredient (mixed with salt for fish or venison), an infusion base (oil or syrup), a tea component (with kawakawa). The plant is recognisable by its distinctive red-spotted leaves — the spots are caused by a fungus, which ironically the plant's own anti-fungal compounds fight.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Horopito connects to the Winteraceae family pepper tradition: Tasmanian pepper (Tasmannia lanceolata) in Australia is the closest relative. Both produce polygodial-based heat. This is a Gondwanan connection — these plants diverged when the landmasses separated. NZ pepper and Australian pepper are botanical cousins separated by the Tasman Sea for 80 million years, both used by indigenous peoples as spice and medicine.

**Flavour Context:**
Horopito heat is front-palate — it hits the tongue immediately, builds to a numbing peak, then fades with a bitter-medicinal finish. It is hotter than black pepper but shorter-lived. Pairs with: venison (the bitterness cuts the richness), fresh fish (a light crust of ground horopito and salt), dark chocolate (OCHO's horopito & kawakawa bar demonstrates the pairing), mānuka honey (the sweet-bitter contrast). Does not pair well with delicate dairy preparations — the bitterness overwhelms.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: Freshly harvested horopito leaves, used within hours. The volatile oils are at peak intensity. GOOD: Dried and ground horopito from a known forager or supplier (Great Taste NZ supplies commercially). ADEQUATE: Commercially packaged dried horopito. INSUFFICIENT: Old, stale dried leaves with no perceptible heat — horopito loses its polygodial potency over time if stored improperly.

**Sensory Tests:**
Aroma: fresh horopito leaves have a sharp, peppery, slightly camphoraceous smell. Taste (on the tongue — a rare case where taste IS the process check): if you bite a fresh horopito leaf and feel no heat within three seconds, the leaf is too old or the wrong species. Visual: the distinctive red-spotted leaves confirm correct identification — critical, as NZ has other pepper-family plants that are not edible. Texture: dried and ground horopito should be fine and uniform; coarse grinding produces uneven heat distribution.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Identification. NZ has multiple plants with peppery properties, and some look similar. The correct species — Pseudowintera colorata — must be confirmed before use. The red-spotted leaves are the visual marker. If the leaves are uniformly green without spots, it may be Pseudowintera axillaris (lowland horopito), which has different potency. The dish lives or dies at the forager's identification.

**Doors:**
→ Horopito (NZ Winteraceae) and Tasmanian pepper (Australian Winteraceae) are Gondwanan cousins — same family, separated by 80 million years of continental drift. → AU-[Tasmannia lanceolata]
→ OCHO Chocolate's horopito & kawakawa bar: NZ native pepper + Pacific-sourced cacao (PNG) in a Dunedin factory. The Pacific Migration Trail expressed in chocolate. → NZ-20 Mānuka, NZ-9
→ Monique Fiso's horopito use at Hiakai is the benchmark modern application. → NZ-12

## NZ-5 — Rēwena Paraoa (Māori Sourdough Potato Bread)

**Cuisine:** Māori | Fermented Bread | Difficulty: 2 | Time: 24–72 hours (starter), 2–3 hours (baking)

**Origin:**
Rēwena paraoa (rēwena = fermented, paraoa = bread/flour) is a Māori sourdough bread leavened with a potato starter called a "bug" or "plant." The technique is a post-contact Māori innovation — flour and potatoes arrived with European settlers, but Māori adapted them using fermentation knowledge that predated contact. The potato starter is made by boiling potatoes, mashing them with sugar and flour, and allowing wild yeasts and lactobacilli to colonise the mixture over two to three days. The resulting starter produces a dense, slightly sweet, tangy bread with a distinctive crumb structure. Rēwena is baked in every marae kitchen in NZ and is the bread served at tangihanga (funerals), hui (meetings), and celebrations. It is one of the few foods that bridges Māori and Pākehā traditions — the technique is Māori, the ingredients are European, and every New Zealander knows what it is.

**Description:**
The potato starter (rēwena bug): boiled potatoes mashed with flour and sugar, left in a warm place for 48–72 hours until it bubbles actively and smells yeasty-sour. The dough: flour, rēwena starter, sugar, sometimes butter. No commercial yeast. The bread is shaped into a round loaf and baked at moderate heat (180°C / 355°F) for 50–70 minutes. The result: a dense, moist, slightly sweet crumb with a mild sourdough tang. The crust is thin and soft — not the thick crust of European sourdough. Rēwena is often eaten warm with butter and jam, or alongside a boil-up (NZ-[boil-up]).

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Rēwena parallels other indigenous adaptations of colonial ingredients: frybread in Native American cuisines (flour, oil, and water transformed into a cultural staple), damper in Indigenous Australian cooking. In each case, colonised peoples took the coloniser's staple ingredients and created something distinct. The fermentation element — wild yeast capture — connects to every sourdough tradition globally, but rēwena's potato base gives it a different microbial profile and flavour from wheat-only starters.

**Flavour Context:**
Rēwena bread has a mild sweetness (from the potato starch and added sugar), a gentle sourdough tang (milder than San Francisco or European sourdough — the potato sugars buffer the acidity), and a dense, moist crumb. It pairs with: butter (the classic — warm rēwena with salted butter is a NZ comfort food), boil-up broth (used to soak up the pork and watercress broth), jam (strawberry or feijoa), or simply on its own. The bread does not stale quickly — the potato starch retains moisture for days.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
EXCEPTIONAL: A marae-baked rēwena from a starter that has been maintained for years — some marae starters are decades old, passed between cooks. The older the starter, the more complex the tang. GOOD: Home-baked rēwena with a well-established starter (1+ week old). ADEQUATE: First-bake rēwena from a fresh starter — the flavour will be milder. INSUFFICIENT: Rēwena made with commercial yeast instead of a potato starter — this misses the point entirely.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: the starter should be actively bubbling, with visible gas production, before use — if it's flat, the fermentation has not established. The baked loaf should have a golden-brown top with a slightly domed shape. Aroma: the starter should smell yeasty-sour, like a sourdough but sweeter. The baked bread should smell of warm bread with a mild tang. Texture: the crumb should be dense and moist — not airy like yeasted bread, not heavy like unleavened bread. The crust should be soft and thin.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
The starter. If the potato bug is not properly fermented — if it has been rushed, or contaminated with unwanted bacteria — the bread will be flat, dense, and sour in the wrong way. A good rēwena starter takes patience: 48–72 hours minimum, fed daily, kept warm. The bread lives at the starter. Everything else is just flour and heat.

**Doors:**
→ Rēwena parallels frybread (Native American) and damper (Indigenous Australian) as a colonial-ingredient adaptation by indigenous peoples. → AU-[damper]
→ The potato-based sourdough starter is a distinct fermentation tradition. The Noma Guide to Fermentation documents similar wild-yeast capture techniques. → [Fermentation canon]
→ Rēwena is the bread of the marae — the same space where manaakitanga is practised. → NZ-42b Rongomaraeroa
