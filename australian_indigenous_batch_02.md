## AI-6 — Wattleseed (Acacia spp. — Seed Grinding Tradition)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — multiple peoples across arid and semi-arid Australia (Arrernte, Warlpiri, Adnyamathanha, Ngarrindjeri) | Seed / Spice | Difficulty: 2 (preparation requires roasting + grinding) | Time: 30–60 minutes

**Origin:**
Wattleseed refers to the seeds of several edible Acacia species native to Australia — a food source with deep archaeological evidence of use. Grinding stones (millstones) dated to 30,000+ years ago have been found at Cuddie Springs (Walgett, NSW), confirming that Aboriginal seed grinding is among the oldest documented food-processing technologies on earth. The primary edible species include Acacia victoriae (elegant wattle, Arrernte: arrwele), Acacia coriacea (wirewood), Acacia aneura (mulga), and Acacia murrayana (colony wattle). Different species were used by different peoples depending on their Country and the species available. The Arrernte of Central Australia used mulga seed; the Adnyamathanha of the Flinders Ranges used A. victoriae. The seeds are ground into flour using paired grinding stones (a flat base stone and a hand-held top stone), mixed with water, and baked into seed cakes on hot coals or hot stones. This tradition is one of the best-corroborated archaeological claims in Australian Indigenous food history (documented by CSIRO, AIATSIS, and confirmed by archaeological evidence at over 140 quarry sites for grinding stones). Coulthard features wattleseed in Warndu Mai (2019) as an Adnyamathanha staple. Woods includes it in Karkalla at Home (2024). Isaacs documents the seed-grinding tradition in Bush Food (1987, revised 2023).

**Description:**
The seeds (hard, small, brown-black, 3–6 mm diameter) are harvested from the pod when mature and dry. Preparation requires three steps: 1. Roasting — the seeds are dry-roasted over coals or in a pan until they darken and become fragrant (this step develops the characteristic nutty-chocolate aroma and neutralises anti-nutritional compounds including trypsin inhibitors). 2. Grinding — the roasted seeds are ground between two stones (or in a modern grinder) into a fine flour. 3. Use — the flour is mixed with water to make a dough and baked into flat seed cakes (damper-style), or used as a flavouring. Contemporary use: roasted wattleseed is ground to a powder and used as a coffee-like beverage (wattleseed "coffee"), as a flavouring in ice cream, chocolate, and baked goods, and as a savoury spice in rubs and sauces. The flavour is described as nutty, chocolate-like, and coffee-like — a result of the Maillard reaction during roasting. Species: Acacia victoriae is the most commercially available; A. coriacea and A. aneura are also used.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Seed-grinding thread: the Aboriginal seed-grinding tradition is one of the oldest on earth and parallels the Neolithic grain-milling traditions of the Fertile Crescent, but predates them by approximately 20,000 years. The technique (harvest → roast → grind → add water → bake) is structurally identical to the processing of wheat, barley, and maize in other traditions. The distinction: Aboriginal seed-grinding was applied to wild Acacia species that were not domesticated (no selective breeding, no planting cycles), managed instead through fire-regime practices that encouraged new growth and seed production. This relationship between fire management and food production is well-documented in the ecological literature and is distinct from agriculture as defined by crop domestication. → Related: AI-5, AI-11, AI-12

**Flavour Context:**
Roasted wattleseed has a complex flavour: nutty (comparable to hazelnut), chocolate (dark, bitter chocolate), coffee (espresso-like roast notes), and a faint earthy, herbal undertone. The flavour is produced by the Maillard reaction during roasting — the same chemical process that produces coffee and chocolate flavours from their raw beans. Raw, unroasted wattleseed has no flavour — the roasting step is essential. Ground wattleseed powder can be used as a 1:1 substitute for cocoa powder in baking, producing a similar colour and bitterness but with a nuttier, more complex flavour. The canonical Australian native flavour trio: wattleseed + bush tomato (AI-5) + lemon myrtle (AI-9) — these three ingredients together produce a flavour profile that is immediately recognisable as Australian.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: wild-harvested seeds from mature Acacia on Country, roasted over coals by an experienced practitioner, ground fresh on stone. Reserve: wild-harvested, commercially roasted and ground by an Indigenous-owned enterprise. Market: cultivated Acacia victoriae seeds, commercially roasted and ground — consistent quality. House: pre-ground wattleseed powder from specialty retailers — convenient but the volatile roast flavours degrade within months of grinding.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: roasted wattleseed is dark brown-black, glossy, approximately 3–6 mm. Ground powder is fine, dark brown, resembling cocoa or fine coffee grounds. Aroma: the defining test — properly roasted wattleseed has an immediate, strong aroma of chocolate, coffee, and hazelnut. If the aroma is flat or dusty, the seed is stale or was under-roasted. Texture: the whole roasted seed is hard — too hard to eat without grinding. The ground powder should be fine enough to dissolve in liquid. Feel: brewed wattleseed "coffee" should have a smooth, slightly bitter character with a nutty aftertaste — no grittiness.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Wattleseed lives or dies on the roasting. Under-roasted seed produces a bland, beany, astringent flour with no complexity. Over-roasted seed (burnt) produces a bitter, acrid, charcoal flavour. The correct roast is dark brown (not black), with an audible crack (similar to the first crack in coffee roasting) that indicates the Maillard reaction is complete. The seed should be fragrant — the nutty-chocolate aroma emerges at the correct roast point and is the primary indicator that the seed is ready for grinding. Raw or under-roasted seed also retains anti-nutritional compounds (trypsin inhibitors) that cause digestive distress.

## AI-7 — Warrigal Greens (Tetragonia tetragonoides — Oxalic Acid Management)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — coastal peoples across southern and eastern Australia (Ngarrindjeri, Kaurna, Wiradjuri, Bundjalung) | Leaf Vegetable / Technique | Difficulty: 1 | Time: 10–15 minutes

**Origin:**
Warrigal greens (Tetragonia tetragonoides, family Aizoaceae — also called New Zealand spinach, Botany Bay greens, or kōkihi in Māori) is a coastal native plant found across southern and eastern Australia, New Zealand, and parts of the Pacific. The species is notable for two reasons: it is one of the few native Australian leafy green vegetables to enter the global food system (Captain Cook's crew ate it to prevent scurvy at Botany Bay in 1770 — Joseph Banks collected specimens and sent seeds to Kew Gardens), and its preparation requires a specific food-safety technique — blanching to remove oxalic acid — that constitutes a genuine technique entry. Oxalic acid in the raw leaves causes kidney irritation and calcium malabsorption if consumed in large quantities. Aboriginal peoples of the coast have eaten warrigal greens for millennia, and the blanching technique (or prolonged cooking) was practised long before European contact. The technique parallels the calcium oxalate management required for taro leaves across the Pacific (FJ-3, WS-3, TO-2). Woods uses warrigal greens in Karkalla at Home (2024) as a native spinach substitute.

**Description:**
Warrigal greens are a spreading, ground-cover plant found on beaches, sand dunes, and coastal margins. The leaves are thick, fleshy, triangular, 3–8 cm long, with a slightly gritty, sand-like surface texture (tiny crystalline cells on the leaf surface). The raw leaves taste similar to spinach but with a pronounced astringent, mouth-drying quality from the oxalic acid content. Preparation technique (the food-safety gate): the leaves are blanched in boiling water for 30 seconds to 2 minutes, then immediately plunged into cold water (ice bath). The blanching water is discarded — it contains the dissolved oxalic acid. The blanched leaves can then be used as a direct substitute for spinach in any preparation: sauteed in butter or oil, added to quiches, used in salads (after blanching), or wilted into soups. The blanching technique is the entry point — without it, the leaves are not safe for regular consumption. After blanching, the flavour is mild, green, and spinach-like, with a slightly more mineral, salty character than European spinach (reflecting the coastal habitat).

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Oxalate-management thread: the blanching technique for warrigal greens parallels the prolonged cooking required for taro leaves in the Pacific (FJ-3, WS-3, TO-2 — where calcium oxalate crystals cause throat irritation if not neutralised by heat). It also connects to the European tradition of blanching sorrel (Rumex acetosa, also high in oxalic acid) before use. The broader principle: identifying a nutritious plant that contains a natural toxin and developing a specific processing technique to render it safe for regular consumption. This is a genuine food-processing innovation, not a simple cooking step. The technique also connects to the cycad nut detoxification process (AI-12), which involves leaching to remove toxic compounds. → Related: FJ-3, WS-3, TO-2, AI-12

**Flavour Context:**
After blanching, warrigal greens have a mild, pleasant, spinach-like flavour with a slightly more mineral and salty character than European spinach (Spinacia oleracea). The leaves are thicker and fleshier than spinach, giving them a more substantial texture. The saltiness reflects the coastal habitat — the plant accumulates sodium from salt spray. This natural saltiness makes warrigal greens self-seasoning in many preparations. The flavour is subtle — warrigal greens are a background vegetable, not a star ingredient. They pair well with butter, garlic, lemon, and strong cheeses. Species: Tetragonia tetragonoides is the only culinary species. The genus Tetragonia contains approximately 60 species, mostly South African; T. tetragonoides is the Australian-New Zealand-Pacific member.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: freshly picked warrigal greens from a coastal garden or wild stand on Country, blanched immediately, served within hours. Reserve: home-grown warrigal greens (the plant is easy to cultivate in temperate and subtropical gardens). Market: purchased from a specialist native-food supplier or farmer's market. House: the plant is not commercially available in supermarkets in most regions — access is the primary quality constraint.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: fresh leaves are thick, fleshy, dark green, with a slightly gritty surface texture (visible crystalline cells). After blanching, the leaves are bright green and tender. Aroma: minimal — a faint, green, herbaceous scent. Texture: blanched warrigal greens are thicker and meatier than spinach — they hold their structure better in cooking and do not collapse into a watery mush. Feel: if the mouth feels dry or puckered after eating, the blanching was insufficient — residual oxalic acid is present. Properly blanched leaves should produce no astringency.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Warrigal greens live or die on the blanching step. This is a mandatory food-safety technique, not a flavour preference. Raw warrigal greens consumed in quantity cause kidney irritation from oxalic acid accumulation. The blanching must be in boiling (not simmering) water, for at least 30 seconds, followed by immediate cold-water shock (which stops the cooking and preserves the bright green colour). The blanching water must be discarded — it contains the dissolved oxalate. If the leaves are simply added to a stir-fry or soup without blanching, the oxalic acid is not removed (it needs to dissolve into the blanching water and be discarded, not just be heated).

## AI-8 — Muntries (Kunzea pomifera — Ngarrindjeri and Kaurna Berry)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — Ngarrindjeri and Kaurna peoples / Coastal South Australia (Coorong, Fleurieu Peninsula, Kangaroo Island) | Native Berry | Difficulty: 1 | Time: minimal

**Origin:**
Muntries (Kunzea pomifera, family Myrtaceae) is a ground-cover plant native to the coastal dunes and cliff-tops of South Australia — Country of the Ngarrindjeri and Kaurna peoples. The plant produces small (5–10 mm diameter), round, green berries that ripen to dark purple-red. The flavour is described as apple-cinnamon — a spiced, sweet-tart character that has no parallel among European fruits. Muntries (also called native cranberries or emu apples) have been eaten by Ngarrindjeri and Kaurna peoples for millennia, gathered from coastal dune systems during the fruiting season (autumn). The berries are eaten fresh, dried for storage, or mixed with other foods. Coulthard (Adnyamathanha, whose Country is further north but who documents South Australian native foods broadly) and Sullivan feature muntries in Warndu Mai (2019). The species is named after Sergeant Muntrij, a police officer, but the Aboriginal names vary by language group (the Kaurna name is recorded as muntrij in colonial-era sources, suggesting the English name may derive from the Kaurna). The plant is now commercially cultivated on a small scale in South Australia.

**Description:**
The berries are small (5–10 mm), round, and produced in clusters on a low, spreading shrub (30–50 cm tall) that forms dense ground cover on coastal dunes. The skin is smooth and transitions from green (unripe) through pink to dark purple-red (fully ripe). The flesh is firm, crisp, and juicy — similar in texture to a small apple or cranberry. The seeds inside are tiny and edible. Eaten fresh: the berries are picked from the bush and eaten as a trail food. Dried: sun-dried or commercially dehydrated for year-round use (the dried berries have a concentrated apple-cinnamon-raisin character). Contemporary use: muntries are used in jams, chutneys, sauces, muffins, and desserts. They pair well with game meats (kangaroo, wallaby) and with cheese. Dried muntries can be used as a substitute for dried cranberries or raisins in baking, bringing a distinctly Australian flavour to familiar recipes.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Native-berry thread: muntries occupy the same functional space as cranberries in North American cooking, lingonberries in Scandinavian cooking, and myrtle berries in Mediterranean cooking. The Myrtaceae family connection is significant — muntrij (Kunzea) is related to lemon myrtle (Backhousia, AI-9), Tasmanian pepperberry (Tasmannia, AI-10, though Tasmannia is Winteraceae, not Myrtaceae), and anise myrtle (Syzygium). Australia's native Myrtaceae contribute a disproportionate number of the continent's edible fruits and flavour plants. → Related: AI-1, AI-5, AI-9, AI-10

**Flavour Context:**
The signature flavour of muntries is apple-cinnamon — a warm, spiced sweetness with a tart acidity. This flavour is produced by natural volatile compounds including methyl cinnamate (the same compound responsible for cinnamon flavour). Fresh muntries have a crisp, juicy texture with balanced sweet-tart flavour. Dried muntries concentrate the cinnamon-spice character and develop raisin-like sweetness. The flavour is warm and autumnal — it evokes spiced apple pie without any added spice. This self-spiced character makes muntries a distinctive ingredient in baking and desserts. The berries also have a faint eucalyptus-myrtaceous note that identifies them as Australian.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: wild-harvested muntries from Coorong or Kangaroo Island coastal dunes, picked at full ripeness (dark purple-red, firm, fragrant). Reserve: cultivated muntries from a South Australian farm, picked in season. Market: frozen or dried muntries from a commercial supplier — the frozen berries retain their texture and flavour well. House: dried muntries from a specialty retailer — convenient and shelf-stable, but the fresh-fruit character is diminished.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: ripe berries are dark purple-red, round, glossy, approximately the size of a blueberry or small marble. Unripe berries (green) are astringent and lack the spiced flavour. Aroma: a warm, spiced, apple-cinnamon scent — immediately recognisable and appealing. Texture: firm, crisp, and juicy — the flesh has a definite crunch (unlike soft berries like raspberry or blueberry). The crunch is part of the experience. Feel: the apple-cinnamon flavour develops on the palate over several seconds — the initial bite is tart, followed by a warm cinnamon sweetness.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Muntries live or die on ripeness. Unripe berries (green or pink) are hard, astringent, and lack the characteristic apple-cinnamon flavour — they taste like sour, unripe apple with none of the warmth. Fully ripe berries (dark purple-red) have the complete flavour profile. The window between ripe and over-ripe is approximately one week — after which the berries soften, ferment, and lose their crisp texture. Commercial harvesting must be timed precisely. Freezing immediately after harvest is the best preservation method — the berries freeze well and retain their crunch after thawing.

## AI-9 — Lemon Myrtle (Backhousia citriodora — Australian Native Citrus Herb)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — Bundjalung, Githabul peoples / Subtropical rainforest, Northern NSW and Southeast Queensland | Native Herb / Spice | Difficulty: 1 | Time: minimal

**Origin:**
Lemon myrtle (Backhousia citriodora, family Myrtaceae) is a native Australian rainforest tree from the subtropical coast of NSW and Queensland — Country of the Bundjalung and Githabul peoples. The leaves contain the highest concentration of citral (the compound responsible for lemon flavour) of any known plant: approximately 90–98% citral in the leaf essential oil, compared to 3–5% in lemon grass and 2–5% in lemon peel. This makes lemon myrtle "more lemon than lemon" — the citrus character is intense, clean, and persistent. Aboriginal peoples of the region used the leaves as a medicine (brewed as tea for colds) and as a food flavouring. The species is now one of the most commercially successful Australian native food plants, cultivated in plantations across subtropical Australia and exported globally as a dried leaf, an essential oil, and a ground spice. Woods (Bundjalung) features lemon myrtle throughout Karkalla at Home (2024) as a foundational herb of her Country. Coulthard and Sullivan include it in Warndu Mai (2019). The species is also documented by Cherikoff in The Bushfood Handbook.

**Description:**
The tree (a medium-sized rainforest tree, 8–20 metres in its natural habitat, smaller in cultivation) produces glossy, dark green, lance-shaped leaves 5–12 cm long. The leaves are harvested, dried, and used as a herb or ground to a fine powder. Fresh leaves can be used whole (added to curries, soups, and sauces, then removed before serving — like bay leaves) or finely chopped. Dried ground lemon myrtle is the most common commercial form — a fine green powder with an intense lemon aroma. The flavour is applied to: biscuits, cakes, and shortbread (lemon myrtle shortbread is one of Australia's most successful native food products); seafood (particularly as a seasoning for fish and prawns); cream-based sauces and desserts (pannacotta, ice cream, cheesecake); and beverages (tea, gin, cocktails). The essential oil is too concentrated to use directly in food — it is diluted or used in very small quantities.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Citrus-herb thread: lemon myrtle performs the same flavour function as lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) in Thai cooking, makrut lime leaf (Citrus hystrix) in Thai and Indonesian cooking, and lemon verbena (Aloysia citrodora) in European and South American cooking — a non-citrus plant that provides intense citrus flavour. The Myrtaceae family connection places lemon myrtle alongside eucalyptus, tea tree, and clove (Syzygium aromaticum) — all aromatic Myrtaceae with high essential-oil content. The commercial success of lemon myrtle as an Australian flavour export parallels the global spread of Australian macadamia nuts (Macadamia integrifolia), which originated in Bundjalung Country and is now cultivated worldwide. → Related: AI-4, AI-8, AI-10

**Flavour Context:**
Lemon myrtle is intensely citric — the highest-citral plant on earth. The flavour is clean, bright, and persistent lemon without the bitterness of lemon pith or the sourness of lemon juice. It is pure lemon aroma and flavour without acid. This makes it structurally different from lemon juice (which adds acid and liquid) — lemon myrtle adds lemon character to dry preparations (baked goods, spice rubs, dry marinades) where lemon juice would be impractical. The dried leaf powder has a slightly eucalyptus-myrtaceous undertone that identifies it as Australian. Overuse produces a medicinal, cleaning-product note (from excess citral) — the powder must be used sparingly. The canonical pairing: lemon myrtle + macadamia + white fish = the archetypal contemporary Australian native flavour combination.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: fresh leaves picked from a Bundjalung Country rainforest tree, used within hours — the citral content is at its peak in fresh leaves. Reserve: air-dried leaves from a plantation, dried slowly to preserve volatile oils. Market: commercially ground lemon myrtle powder from a certified Australian supplier — the standard form for most culinary use. House: lemon myrtle essential oil used in food (requires precise dilution — undiluted oil causes chemical burns and is toxic in large quantities).

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: fresh leaves are glossy, dark green, lance-shaped. Dried ground powder is bright green (fading to olive-green with age). If the powder is brown, it is stale. Aroma: the primary quality test — rub the leaf or powder between fingers and smell. The aroma should be intensely, immediately lemony — stronger than lemon zest, stronger than lemongrass. If the aroma is faint or musty, the product is old. Texture: the ground powder should be fine and free-flowing, not clumped (clumping indicates moisture absorption). Feel: a fresh leaf chewed produces an immediate, intense citrus-lemon sensation that fills the mouth and persists for minutes.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Lemon myrtle lives or dies on quantity and freshness. Too much lemon myrtle in a dish crosses the line from pleasant citrus to medicinal/cleaning-product — the citral overwhelms everything else and the eucalyptus-myrtaceous undertone becomes dominant. The correct quantity is approximately half a teaspoon of ground powder per 4 servings (for baked goods) or less (for delicate preparations like pannacotta). The second pivot: freshness. Citral is volatile and degrades with exposure to light, heat, and air. Stale lemon myrtle has a flat, musty, hay-like character that is nothing like the fresh product. Ground powder should be stored in an opaque, airtight container and used within 6 months.

## AI-10 — Pepperberry / Mountain Pepper (Tasmannia lanceolata — Southeastern Australian Spice)

**Cuisine:** Australian Indigenous — Palawa (Tasmanian Aboriginal) peoples, southeastern mainland peoples / Tasmania, Victoria, NSW highlands | Native Spice | Difficulty: 1 | Time: minimal

**Origin:**
Mountain pepper (Tasmannia lanceolata, family Winteraceae) is a native Australian shrub from the cool temperate rainforests and alpine regions of Tasmania, Victoria, and highland NSW. Both the berries and the leaves are used as a spice. The Palawa (Tasmanian Aboriginal) peoples used mountain pepper as food and medicine — the berries as a flavouring and the leaves as a therapeutic preparation. The species was also used by Aboriginal peoples of the southeast mainland. Mountain pepper is one of the earliest Australian native foods to be documented by European botanists — it was collected by Banks and Solander on Cook's 1770 voyage. The species name Tasmannia honours Abel Tasman. The berries (small, 5–8 mm, black when ripe) contain polygodial, a sesquiterpene compound that produces a pungent, peppery heat — similar in sensation to Sichuan pepper (Zanthoxylum) rather than to black pepper (Piper nigrum). The leaves are also peppery, with a more herbaceous, eucalyptus-like character. Coulthard and Sullivan feature mountain pepper in Warndu Mai (2019). Woods includes it in Karkalla at Home (2024). CSIRO has documented the species through the Australian National Herbarium.

**Description:**
The plant is a shrub or small tree (2–5 metres in cultivation, up to 10 metres in rainforest) with dark green, aromatic leaves and small, dark berries. The berries are harvested when fully ripe (dark purple-black, autumn to winter) and dried. The leaves are harvested year-round and dried. Both are sold commercially as whole dried berries, ground berry powder, or dried leaf flakes. The flavour is peppery and complex — not a simple heat like chilli, but a layered spice experience that begins with a fruity, berry-like sweetness, transitions to a sharp, woody, peppery heat, and finishes with a numbing, tingling sensation (similar to Sichuan pepper). The leaf has a more herbaceous character — peppery with eucalyptus and camphor notes. Use: mountain pepper berry is used as a substitute for black pepper in European-style cooking, as a rub for game meats (kangaroo, venison, duck), in sausages and charcuterie, and in chocolate. The leaf is used in sauces, stocks, and as a garnish.

**Thread (Cross-Cuisine):**
Pepper-spice thread: mountain pepper connects to the global family of pungent spice plants that provide heat through different chemical compounds. Black pepper (Piper nigrum): piperine. Sichuan pepper (Zanthoxylum): hydroxy-alpha-sanshool. Mountain pepper (Tasmannia lanceolata): polygodial. Chilli (Capsicum spp.): capsaicin. Each produces a different quality of heat — mountain pepper's heat is woody, numbing, and short-lived, closest to Sichuan pepper in character. The Winteraceae family (which includes Tasmannia) is one of the most ancient angiosperm families — fossil evidence suggests it predates the breakup of Gondwana. Mountain pepper is a literally ancient spice. → Related: AI-5, AI-8, AI-9, NZ-4

**Flavour Context:**
Mountain pepper berry has a three-stage flavour: Stage 1 (first 2 seconds) — a fruity, berry-like sweetness with a faint juniper-like aromatic character. Stage 2 (seconds 3–10) — a sharp, woody, peppery heat that builds rapidly and fills the mouth. Stage 3 (seconds 10–30) — a numbing, tingling sensation on the tongue and lips that is reminiscent of Sichuan pepper. The heat dissipates after approximately 1 minute. The leaf has a similar but more herbaceous profile — the eucalyptus-camphor notes from the leaf's essential oil add a cooling dimension. The heat level is moderate — comparable to mild-to-medium black pepper, but the quality of the heat is more complex. Key pairing: mountain pepper + kangaroo is the canonical Australian native meat-and-spice combination.

**Quality Hierarchy:**
Exceptional: wild-harvested berries from Tasmanian cool temperate rainforest, dried slowly at low temperature to preserve polygodial and aromatic compounds. Reserve: cultivated berries from a Tasmanian or Victorian plantation, professionally dried. Market: commercially produced ground mountain pepper from a certified supplier. House: the ground product degrades with time (polygodial is volatile) — stale mountain pepper loses its heat and complexity.

**Sensory Tests:**
Visual: dried berries are small (5–8 mm), dark purple-black, wrinkled. Dried leaves are dark green to olive, curled. The ground powder is dark grey-brown. Aroma: complex — woody, peppery, with a faint eucalyptus and camphor note. Fresh-ground powder has an immediate, sharp aromatic punch. If the aroma is faint, the product is old. Texture: the dried berries are hard — they need to be ground in a pepper mill or spice grinder. The leaf is brittle and crumbles to flakes. Feel: the three-stage flavour progression (sweet → hot → numb) is the defining sensory test. If the berry produces no numbing sensation, it is stale or was harvested immature.

**Where the Dish Lives or Dies:**
Mountain pepper lives or dies on heat management in cooking. Polygodial (the pungent compound) is heat-sensitive — it degrades at temperatures above 35°C / 95°F. This means mountain pepper must be added at the end of cooking (or used as a finishing spice), never cooked for extended periods. If added to a hot pan or simmered in a sauce, the heat compound breaks down and the pepper becomes a bland, woody, flavourless addition. This is the opposite of black pepper (where heat is stable) and is the most important technical fact about using mountain pepper. The pepper must be ground fresh — pre-ground powder loses its polygodial within weeks.
